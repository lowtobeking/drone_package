## 0. 定位声明（写作前必读，不进正文）

`report/阶段性报告.md` 里"接口一致 MPC"的技术手法（辨识内环一阶滞后 → 增广进 MPC 状态方程）**不是新方法**：

- **Kamel, Burri & Siegwart (IFAC-PapersOnLine, 2017 / arXiv:1611.09240)** — 单机六旋翼真机，辨识姿态环一阶滞后 τ_φ, τ_θ（方程 4a/4b），直接增广进 NMPC 状态向量（方程 5, 13）。**全文已核实**：辨识结果确实被写进状态空间模型用于多步预测，机制与本工作对速度环做的事完全同构。
- **Stastny & Siegwart (ICUAS 2018)** — 单机固定翼真机，标题即为 "Nonlinear Model Predictive Guidance for Fixed-wing UAVs Using **Identified Control Augmented Dynamics**"，方法论与本工作几乎一致。
- **PX4 官方文档**：`MPC_ACC_HOR` 调参指南明确写"实际速度会滞后于设定点，且滞后随该参数增大"；`CP_DELAY`（碰撞规避延迟）文档给出滞后典型范围 **0.1–0.5 s**，与本工作实测 τ≈0.48 s（风下 0.61 s）几乎精确落在同一量级——证实"PX4 速度环滞后"是社区已知现象，本工作的辨识数值也因此获得外部佐证。

**因此本文的贡献边界重新划定为**：

1. **诊断**：首次在**分布式编队 MPC**框架下，形式化定位并量化"队形耦合代价项将单机已知的速度环滞后失配放大为跨机极限环"这一交互式故障模式（单机文献 [Kamel16, Stastny18] 未覆盖多机耦合）。
2. **否定性结果**：扰动观测器路线（在 130 Hz 实测数据上对 1.0 m/s² 注入风仅估出 0.017 m/s²）对此类"接口失配型"误差从机制上无效，区别于外部扰动型误差——用于对打 DOB-MPC 一类文献（如 [Xu24]）。
3. **系统性修复与验证**：接口一致建模（沿用 [Kamel16, Stastny18] 的辨识+增广技巧，首次系统应用于速度环 + 分布式多机场景）+ 41 场景全回归 + "趴窝队友"编队拽拉这一独立缺陷的发现与修复。

**摘要/Introduction 措辞禁忌**：不能写 "we propose interface-consistent MPC"（会被 [Kamel16]/[Stastny18] 拒稿）；应写 "we identify and formalize a previously unreported multi-vehicle failure mode caused by a well-known single-vehicle interface mismatch..."。

## 1. Title & Abstract

**Title**（2026-08-14 定稿）：

> **Interface Mismatch, Not Disturbance: Diagnosing and Fixing a Velocity-Loop Lag in PX4 Offboard MPC**

**Abstract**（草稿，~250 词；2026-08-13 压缩+对齐 M1/M3/m5/m6 修改；数字/结论均取自报告 §5.G/§5.H，未新增声称）：

> Distributed model predictive control (MPC) for multi-UAV formation flight typically assumes each vehicle's low-level velocity controller realizes commanded setpoints instantaneously. We report a case study in which this assumption — individually documented within the PX4 ecosystem and previously addressed for single vehicles — produces an unreported failure mode when the planner is a model predictive controller whose internal model asserts the lag away: not degraded tracking, but a saturation-driven instability. During software-in-the-loop (SITL) regression of a one-to-nine-vehicle acados-based distributed MPC swarm on PX4 v1.14, circular and wind-forced trajectories showed intermittent tracking degradation that survived two remedial hypotheses: removing an outer proportional loop (suspected algorithmic oscillation) and an online disturbance observer (suspected external forcing), the latter recovering only 0.017 m/s² of a 1.0 m/s² injected disturbance. Offline 130 Hz identification instead revealed a first-order velocity-loop lag $\tau\approx0.48$ s (still-air velocity-reconstruction $R^2=0.999$), consistent with PX4's documented 0.1–0.5 s tracking delay; the MPC's zero-lag model overestimates its acceleration authority by an order of magnitude, producing a saturation-bounded limit cycle unforced and large-amplitude divergence under wind. We further report — and falsify — a third hypothesis of our own: that a persistent formation-keeping cost amplifies this single-vehicle mismatch across a swarm. The divergence does roughly double in a five-vehicle simulation, but a controlled decomposition shows neither the formation-keeping weight ($p=0.55$) nor the collision term ($p=0.84$) accounts for it, and the doubling is already present with *all* explicit inter-vehicle coupling removed ($p=0.008$ against the single vehicle); we therefore make no coupling claim. We also show the failure is not a matter of low-level tuning: tripling the velocity-loop gain improves nominal tracking by 38 % while leaving the wind-forced divergence statistically unchanged. We argue this failure mode is ill-suited to additive disturbance-observer compensation and mechanistically distinct from the exogenous disturbances such observers target. Folding the identified lag into the OCP's prediction model — a known single-vehicle technique, here carried across to a distributed formation swarm — eliminates it in SITL (wind-forced peak-to-peak error reduced by two-to-three orders of magnitude; unforced tracking error 0.368→0.035 m), and passes a 41-scenario regression suite without side effects. Validation is presently SITL-only.

## 2. Introduction

**Para 1 — 背景**（草稿）：

> Distributed model predictive control (MPC) has become a standard approach to multi-UAV formation flight: each vehicle solves a local optimal control problem and couples to its neighbours through broadcast state, trajectory, or barrier-function information [LiuSurvey24], [XuAccess23], [Goarin24]. PX4 has emerged as a de facto standard flight-control stack for carrying such architectures from simulation to hardware. This paper concerns a distributed acados-based MPC swarm, built on this common template, that we validated through simulation-in-the-loop (SITL) regression across formations of one to nine vehicles. We present it deliberately as a **single-system case study**: the phenomenon is described in the general language of distributed formation MPC, but all evidence — and therefore every quantitative claim — comes from this one PX4-based stack (one MPC formulation, one solver, one weight set, one firmware version), with no real-flight validation. We are correspondingly careful to separate what is *demonstrated here* from what is *conjectured to generalize*, and state the boundary explicitly in §7.2.

**Para 2 — 已知但被忽视的接口问题**（草稿）：

> A common simplifying assumption in these formulations is that the low-level velocity controller realizes a commanded setpoint instantaneously. This assumption is imperfect, and the imperfection is not new information: PX4's own tuning documentation states that realized velocity lags the setpoint, with the lag increasing with the `MPC_ACC_HOR` parameter, and its collision-prevention module budgets a tracking delay in the range of 0.1–0.5 s [PX4Docs]. For single vehicles, identifying this lag from flight data and augmenting it into the MPC's internal prediction model is an established technique: [Kamel16] identify a first-order closed-loop attitude response and augment it into an NMPC state vector on a real hexacopter; [Stastny18] identify a full closed-loop/open-loop grey-box model — including an explicit throttle-lag time constant — for a fixed-wing NMPC guidance law, again validated on real hardware. **We do not claim novelty in this augmentation technique**; we adopt it.

**Para 3 — 本文发现的缺口**（草稿）：

> The works above are single-vehicle. In this paper we report what that mismatch does when the planner is a formation MPC whose internal model asserts the lag away: not degraded tracking, but a saturation-driven instability (a limit cycle unforced, a divergence under wind) that is already present on a single vehicle, roughly doubles in a five-vehicle formation, and is not removed by retuning the low-level loop. We observed this while performing routine SITL regression on a 5-to-9-vehicle formation; our first hypothesis — that the observed oscillation was an artifact of an outer control loop — was falsified by removing that loop, after which the oscillation persisted under wind disturbance. Our second hypothesis — that an unmodelled exogenous disturbance was responsible — was falsified by constructing and testing an online disturbance observer, which recovered only 1.7 % of an injected disturbance's magnitude. We show that this negative result is not an artifact of a poorly tuned observer but a consequence of the true root cause: the mismatch lies in the mapping from the MPC's velocity command to the realized velocity (an input-channel dynamic), whose equivalent disturbance is state-dependent and fast — violating the quasi-static-disturbance assumption on which additive DOB-MPC compensation of the FxTDO family relies, so that such compensation is ill-suited to it regardless of how accurately the (largely DC-cancelled) residual is estimated. This distinguishes the failure mode from the exogenous disturbances addressed by disturbance-observer-based MPC (DOB-MPC) literature such as [Xu24]; a direct closed-loop DOB-MPC comparison confirms it in §6.5 — an additive observer leaves the divergence statistically intact at any tuning, because the quantity it estimates is the command, not a disturbance.

**Para 4 — 贡献列表**（草稿，对应"定位声明"三条）：

> The contributions of this paper are:
> 1. **Diagnosis.** We identify, formalize, and quantify — to our knowledge for the first time in distributed formation MPC — a failure mode in which a well-documented single-vehicle velocity-loop lag, asserted to be zero by the planner's internal model, produces not degraded tracking but a saturation-driven instability (a saturation-bounded limit cycle without wind, and a large-amplitude divergence under wind), and one that retuning the low-level velocity loop does not remove (§6.2d). The failure is absent from prior work, which either pairs the same lag-identification technique with a *reactive*, non-formation coupling scheme [KamelIROS17] or pairs a formation-keeping cost with an *unidentified* nominal model [Erunsal19].
> 2. **Negative result.** We show — empirically (an online observer recovers only 0.017 m/s² of a 1.0 m/s² injected disturbance; a matched closed-loop DOB-MPC comparison leaves the wind-forced divergence statistically unchanged at both a conservative and an aggressive observer cap, $n=5$, §6.5) and by an assumption-level argument (an input-channel lag produces a state-dependent, fast equivalent disturbance that violates the quasi-static assumption of additive compensation, while its DC part is already cancelled by the velocity loop's integral action, §3.3) — that this failure is ill-suited to additive disturbance-observer compensation and is mechanistically distinct from the exogenous disturbances such methods target.
> 3. **Fix and validation.** We fold the identified lag into the OCP's own prediction model — an established single-vehicle technique, which we adopt unmodified — and validate it at a scale not previously reported for it, one to nine vehicles across 41 scenarios: wind-forced peak-to-peak tracking error falls from a median of 153 m to $0.612\pm0.022$ m and unforced circular tracking error from $0.359\pm0.002$ m to $0.035\pm0.001$ m (both $n=5$; see §6.2b for the metric definition, and §6.2 for the corresponding single-run along-wind figures); $n\ge5$ repeated-trial statistics and a $\tau$-sensitivity sweep confirm the effect is not a single-trial artifact; and the fix passes a 41-scenario regression suite (41/41) with no observed side effects — evidence of harmlessness across the operating envelope rather than of efficacy, which rests on the controlled comparisons above.
> 4. **A design condition with a stability certificate.** From the MPC's own cost weights alone we derive a closed-form damping margin $\zeta(\tau,\lambda)$ and, treating the velocity-command saturation as a sector-$[0,1]$ nonlinearity, a circle-criterion condition $\tau(k_p+w_f\lambda)<k_v+1$ under which the interface-consistent loop is *absolutely* stable — giving an admissible plant lag of 1.06 s for an isolated vehicle down to 0.72 s for the densest graph tested, against an identified $\tau\approx0.48$ s (§5.6). We ground $\zeta$ in the classical delay-margin-versus-Laplacian-eigenvalue theory of networked consensus and establish the nominal stability of the fix. We present these as *design conditions*, not as empirically validated predictions: our controlled topology sweep does not recover the ordering they imply — `star5` and `cross5` come out transposed, and the one pair that holds fleet size fixed resolves in neither direction — while severity instead tracks fleet size (§5.6).

**Novelty statement（显式创新点声明，草稿；2026-08-13 新增，回应"把已有贡献拔高为创新点"）：**

> **What is and is not new.** The *only* element we do not claim as novel is the lag-identification-and-state-augmentation technique itself, adopted without modification from the single-vehicle literature [Kamel16], [Stastny18]. The contribution lies not in that technique but in three respects that, to our knowledge, prior work does not reach. **(N1) A failure mode, not a tracking penalty.** A velocity-loop lag that the planner's model asserts to be zero does not merely degrade tracking: it produces a *saturation-driven divergence* under wind (median 49 m on a single vehicle), and — the part we have not seen reported — one that low-level retuning does not touch. Tripling the velocity-loop gain measurably improves nominal tracking (38 %, $p=0.036$) while leaving the divergence statistically unchanged ($p=0.39$, $p=0.57$; §6.2d), because a model asserting $\tau=0$ is wrong by 100 % whatever the true lag is. Nor has the effect's *localization* been reported, established here by a non-predictive consensus baseline (§6.5), to the predictive controller's lag-blind internal model rather than to the plant, the coupling, or velocity saturation. **(N2) A structural negative result.** We give what is, to our knowledge, the first explicit argument — with a matched closed-loop confirmation (§6.5) — that an *input-channel* lag is ill-suited to additive disturbance-observer compensation, cleanly separating interface-mismatch error from the exogenous-disturbance error that DOB-MPC and offset-free MPC target. **(N3) A design condition with an absolute-stability certificate.** The closed-form damping margin $\zeta(\tau,\lambda)$, the circle-criterion condition $\tau(k_p+w_f\lambda)<k_v+1$ that certifies the interface-consistent loop against the velocity-command saturation, and their grounding in the classical delay-margin-versus-Laplacian-eigenvalue theory of consensus (§5.6) give a checkable design rule — including, for a heterogeneous fleet, that it is the *largest* per-vehicle lag that binds. We claim these as design conditions only, and report that our own controlled sweep declines to support the ordering they predict: $\lambda_{\max}$ co-varies with fleet size across the graphs tested, the two five-vehicle graphs come out transposed relative to the prediction, and the observed severity tracks fleet size instead (§5.6). We deliberately locate the contribution in the *diagnosis, the assumption-level negative result, the predictive condition, and the system-level demonstration* rather than in the low-level technique, because — as §3.2 establishes in detail — a bare "new augmentation method" claim would not survive comparison with [Kamel16]/[Stastny18], whereas N1–N3 have no such precedent. Separately, and *not* counted among these, we report an implementation-level defect surfaced by the validation regression (the "parked-teammate pull," §6.4) as a practitioner war-story rather than a general finding.

**Para 5 — 论文结构导航**（草稿）：

> The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. §3 positions this work against distributed formation MPC, single-vehicle lag-augmented MPC, and DOB-MPC literature. §4 summarizes the system under study. §5 presents the diagnosis as a sequence of falsified and confirmed hypotheses. §6 presents the fix and its SITL validation. §7 discusses limitations and practical implications, and §8 concludes.

## 3. Related Work

We position this paper against three lines of work.

### 3.1 分布式/去中心化编队 MPC（**已完成检索，2026-07-15**）

**正文草稿：**

> Distributed formation MPC is a mature area; [LiuSurvey24] survey position-, displacement-, distance-, and bearing-based distributed formation methods across UAV, UGV, USV, and AUV platforms, and a complementary recent review [PengReview24] surveys distributed MPC across UAV and vehicle-platoon systems. Among recent representative instances, [XuAccess23] propose a two-layer distributed MPC for leader-follower multi-UAV formation in which each follower reconstructs an assumed predicted trajectory for its neighbours from their last-broadcast state and control sequence — a coupling mechanism structurally close to the one used in this paper (§4) — but validate it only in MATLAB numerical simulation (`fmincon`, no ROS/Gazebo/flight-control firmware, no hardware, one leader plus two followers). The same broadcast-coupling family has been extended to explicit communication anomalies: [LiHu25] maintain formation under delay and packet loss by propagating *assumed prediction states* — structurally the coupling we study, and directly relevant to the communication-dropout scenarios in our regression (§6.3). [XuDai25] give a Lyapunov-based distributed MPC — each vehicle running a three-layer hierarchical controller — for dynamic virtual-leader formation tracking that is fault-tolerant to unexpected actuator faults; we contrast with it in §6.4, where an agent's *silent* failure exposes a different, formation-cost-driven defect that a fault-tolerant design does not target. [Goarin24] propose a decentralized NMPC with exponential control-barrier-function (ECBF) constraints for collision avoidance among independently-goaled quadrotor teams, validated in simulation with up to ten vehicles and on hardware with three; its coupling mechanism (pairwise safety constraints, not a broadcast predicted trajectory or a persistent formation-keeping term) differs from ours, so we cite it as a same-period decentralized-NMPC effort and as evidence that this general research direction has begun reaching real hardware — a point we return to in our limitations (§7.2), since our own validation is presently SITL-only. Crucially, even the most recent representative distributed formation MPC is still built on a nominal vehicle model with no firmware-in-the-loop execution interface: [Drones25] (2025; formation and obstacle soft costs, simulation only) occupies the same cell of Table I as [Erunsal19], so the gap this paper addresses is not a historical artifact. The system architecture used in this paper (double-integrator dynamics, tracking/formation/collision-avoidance soft costs, SQP-RTI, broadcast-predicted-trajectory coupling; §4) is a conventional instance of this general framework and is **not claimed as a contribution**; it is described only to the extent needed to support the diagnosis in §5.

### 3.2 内环动态辨识增广进 MPC（单机 / 多机但非编队）

**正文草稿：**

> Identifying a low-level controller's closed-loop or actuator dynamics from flight data and augmenting it into an MPC's internal prediction model is an established single-vehicle technique. [Kamel16] identify a first-order closed-loop attitude response ($\tau_\phi$, $\tau_\theta$) on a real hexacopter and augment it directly into an NMPC state vector. [Stastny18] identify a considerably richer grey-box model for a fixed-wing platform — coupled lateral-directional/longitudinal closed-loop attitude dynamics (ten parameters, fitted and validated with a train/validation/test split and reported RMSE) plus an open-loop velocity-axis model with an explicit first-order throttle-lag time constant — using the MATLAB System Identification Toolbox on real flight data. **This methodological rigor exceeds our own $\tau$-identification procedure** (a single first-order fit from a 130 Hz measurement bypass, §5.4), a limitation we return to in §7.2. [KamelIROS17] extend the same identification-and-augmentation technique to a *multi*-vehicle setting — simulating six MAVs and flying two real AscTec NEO hexacopters — but for reactive, independent-goal collision avoidance rather than formation keeping: there is no virtual leader or geometric shape constraint, and neighbour coupling uses a constant-velocity extrapolation of the neighbour's last known state (Eq. 15 in their paper) rather than a broadcast predicted trajectory. We verified against the full text that [KamelIROS17] does not analyze any interaction between the identified lag and the multi-vehicle avoidance constraints; the lag model appears only in a single-vehicle tracking context, and no oscillation, limit-cycle, or stability analysis is reported for the coupled case. [Erunsal19] instead study true formation keeping — a centralized leader-follower scheme with fixed relative-position references among three vehicles (one physical leader, two followers) — but use only a nominal six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body model with no identified low-level dynamics; an oscillation observed in their simulation is explicitly attributed to sensor noise and model-parameter uncertainty, not to an interface mismatch, and no augmentation or coupling-induced instability is analyzed.
>
> These two papers are complementary rather than overlapping evidence for the gap this paper addresses (Table I). [KamelIROS17] identify and augment the lag but couple reactively; [Erunsal19] couple through a persistent formation cost but do not identify or augment any lag. To our knowledge, no prior work combines the two — identifying and augmenting a low-level lag *and* coupling agents through a persistent formation-keeping cost — which is the architecture in which we observe the failure reported in §5.1. We stress that this is a statement about where the failure was found, not about what causes it: §6.2c shows the divergence survives with every explicit inter-vehicle coupling removed. More broadly, compensating for input delay and actuator lag inside MPC is a well-established topic in its own right (dead-time compensation, Smith-predictor-augmented MPC, and delay-state augmentation), which further underlines that we make no novelty claim for the compensation technique itself; our contribution is the multi-vehicle *diagnosis*, not the single-vehicle *remedy*.
>
> **The identification-and-learning landscape.** Two threads bracket our own single-fit $\tau$-identification. First, *parametric low-level identification*: [Eschmann24] identify a quadrotor's inertia, thrust curve, and — closest to our concern — a **first-order motor delay** purely from proprioceptive flight data, noting that such low-level delays, though crucial for end-to-end control, are routinely neglected; this is a more recent, same-platform (multirotor, first-order delay) benchmark than [Stastny18], and we return to it in §7.2 as the methodological standard our single 130 Hz fit falls short of. Second, *learned-residual* approaches replace parametric augmentation with a data-driven correction: [Torrente21] fold a Gaussian-process residual into a quadrotor MPC (>100 Hz, up to 70 % tracking-error reduction over the nominal model), and [Kulathunga24] model precisely the residual between a high-level planner and a low-level controller — focusing on the velocity-change (acceleration) channel, the same interface we diagnose — with a sparse GP. We adopt parametric identify-and-augment rather than GP learning for its zero added inference cost and its interpretability (a single physical time constant), but note the learned-residual route as complementary evidence that this planner↔execution interface is worth modelling explicitly (§7.1).

### 3.3 扰动观测器 + MPC（DOB-MPC）

**正文草稿：**

> A different literature line responds to tracking error by estimating and compensating for an exogenous disturbance. [Xu24] propose a fixed-time disturbance observer (FxTDO) that estimates a lumped additive force disturbance $\hat f_d$ in the translational dynamics ($\dot v = \dots + \hat f_d/m$) and feeds it forward into the MPC's prediction model, validated on real hardware under wind and unknown payloads; their observer is explicitly designed for disturbances that are bounded with a bounded derivative (Assumption 1 in their paper) — i.e., slowly varying or quasi-static forcing — and does not target impulsive or high-frequency disturbances. A related extended-state-observer-enhanced LPV-MPC design [NigusieAST26] reports robustness to actuator/motor lags on the order of $\tau=0.03$–$0.08$ s (alongside MEMS sensor noise and Dryden turbulence, single quadrotor, simulation only), roughly an order of magnitude smaller than the $\tau\approx0.48$–$0.65$ s we identify (§5.4) — consistent with the view that small lags fall within reach of ESO-style lumped-disturbance compensation while larger ones (and, more fundamentally, any lag treated as a state-equation disturbance rather than an input-channel remapping) do not; like [Xu24], this design is single-vehicle and reports no formation-coupling interaction. This line remains active: as recently as 2025, [SciChinaGNN25] pair a graph-neural-network wind-effect predictor with a disturbance-aware quadrotor MPC — so the estimate-and-compensate paradigm we contrast against is a current, evolving baseline rather than a straw man.
>
> We do not merely report that our own observer failed empirically (§5.3); we argue *why this class of method is ill-suited* here — taking care not to overstate the argument as an information-theoretic impossibility. FxTDO-style observers model the mismatch as an *additive force term in the state equation* and, critically, assume it is slowly varying relative to the observer/horizon bandwidth (bounded derivative, [Xu24, Assumption 1]; held constant across the prediction horizon). The root cause we identify (§5.4) is instead a mismatch in the *input channel itself*: the MPC's model assumes $\dot v = u$, while the true plant obeys $\dot v = (v_{\mathrm{sp}}-v)/\tau$. The corresponding equivalent disturbance $d_{\mathrm{eq}} = \dot v - u$ is neither exogenous nor quasi-static — it is a state-dependent signal that changes with the commanded and realized velocity at every step — so a compensator that holds $\hat f_d$ constant over the horizon cannot represent it, while its steady-state component is already cancelled by the velocity loop's own integral action (§5.3), leaving essentially nothing for the observer to lock onto at DC. We do **not** claim that no observer whatsoever could help: a sufficiently high-bandwidth estimator of a fast, time-varying $d_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is not ruled out in principle. Nor do we claim the result extends to every adaptive scheme: adaptive-NMPC designs such as [Hanover22] reject an unmodelled payload or aerodynamic force through an L1-style estimate of a *state-equation* disturbance, and our argument concerns exactly that additive-in-the-state-equation class — not the input-channel remapping it does not perform. We scope N2 to that class deliberately. The claim is narrower and, we argue, more useful — the specific quasi-static-disturbance assumption that FxTDO-family DOB-MPC relies on is *violated* by an input-channel lag, so the principled correction is to re-specify the input channel itself: the state-augmentation technique of [Kamel16] and [Stastny18], which we adopt in §6. The same conclusion extends beyond the FxTDO family to the broader plant–model-mismatch MPC literature: offset-free MPC augments the model with integrating *disturbance* states to reject a constant or asymptotically-constant mismatch and eliminate steady-state offset [PannocchiaRawlings03], but this too is an additive state-equation disturbance aimed at an *offset* rather than at an input-channel remapping, and — being predicated on a slowly-varying disturbance — falls under the same argument. Our failure is not a steady-state offset but a saturation-driven limit cycle, so integrating-disturbance/offset-free augmentation does not address it. A direct closed-loop DOB-MPC comparison confirms this in §6.5: an additive observer leaves the wind-forced divergence statistically intact at both a conservative and an aggressive cap, exactly as the command-correlated (not exogenous) nature of the residual predicts.

## 4. System Description（压缩自报告 §2–§3）

**正文草稿：**

> **Plant and low-level control architecture.** The controlled plant is a multirotor (a Holybro X500-class quadrotor, 500 mm wheelbase, in our PX4 v1.14 / Gazebo SITL). Its translational rigid-body dynamics are
> $$ m\,\ddot p = f_{\mathrm{th}}\,R(\Theta)\,e_3 - m g\,e_3 + f_{\mathrm{ext}}, $$
> where $m$ is the vehicle mass, $f_{\mathrm{th}}\ge0$ the collective thrust, $R(\Theta)\in SO(3)$ the body-to-world rotation, $e_3=[0,0,1]^\top$, $g$ gravity, and $f_{\mathrm{ext}}$ external forces (e.g. wind). PX4 does **not** expose $f_{\mathrm{th}}$ or $\Theta$ to the MPC; it runs a cascade of closed loops — an outer position/velocity controller emitting a thrust-and-attitude setpoint, an attitude loop, an angular-rate loop, and control allocation to the rotors. Commanded in offboard velocity mode (`OffboardControlMode.velocity`), the MPC supplies only a velocity setpoint $v_{\mathrm{cmd}}$, so the entire inner cascade (velocity $\to$ attitude $\to$ rate $\to$ motor) is a *closed-loop subsystem* $\mathcal G:\,v_{\mathrm{cmd}}\mapsto v$ interposed between the MPC and the rigid body. **The plant the MPC actually drives is $\mathcal G$, not the raw rigid body.** The MPC's internal model assumes $\mathcal G$ is the identity (instantaneous velocity realization, $\dot v=u$); §5.4 identifies it instead as a first-order lag, $\dot v=(v_{\mathrm{cmd}}-v)/\tau$ with $\tau\approx0.48$ s, and this paper concerns the consequences of that single modelling gap. We deliberately keep the MPC's own prediction model a double integrator (below), because that assumption is precisely what is under scrutiny.
>
> Each of $N$ ($N\in\{1,\dots,9\}$ in our SITL evaluation) UAVs runs an independent double-integrator MPC over this interface. Per-agent state $x=[p^\top,v^\top]^\top\in\mathbb R^6$ (world-frame NED position and velocity) evolves under the MPC's *assumed* dynamics $\dot p=v,\ \dot v=u$, with $u\in\mathbb R^3$ the commanded acceleration. Each agent solves, at 50 Hz with a receding horizon of $N_h=30$ steps at $\Delta t=0.05$ s ($T=1.5$ s), the nonlinear least-squares OCP
> $$
> \min_{x_{0:N_h},\,u_{0:N_h-1}} \tfrac12\sum_{k=0}^{N_h-1}\Big[q_p\lVert p_k-p_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert^2 + q_v\lVert v_k-v_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert^2 + r_a\lVert u_k\rVert^2 + w_c\!\!\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}\!\big[\max(0,d_{\mathrm{safe}}-d_{ij,k})\big]^2 + w_f\!\!\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}\!(d_{ij,k}-d_{ij}^\star)^2\Big] + \text{(terminal cost)}
> $$
> subject to the discretized dynamics and box constraints on $u$ and $v$, solved with acados [Verschueren22] (`SQP_RTI`, `PARTIAL_CONDENSING_HPIPM`). The reference $p_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$ tracks a broadcast virtual leader (with a second-order feedforward term for circular trajectories) plus a static formation offset; the collision term $d_{\mathrm{safe}}$ is a hinge penalty with $w_c=200\gg w_f=0.5$, so collision avoidance strictly dominates formation keeping. **Neighbours are coupled by broadcasting each agent's own predicted position trajectory** $\{p_{j,k}\}_{k=0}^{N_h}$, which is substituted into $d_{ij,k}$ and $d_{ij}^\star$ in place of a single current-position estimate; this is the coupling mechanism referenced in §3.1's comparison with [XuAccess23].
>
> **Interface under study.** The OCP's solution is not applied to the plant directly. PX4 is commanded in velocity-control mode (`OffboardControlMode.velocity=True`): the horizontal components of the MPC's *predicted* state one step ahead, $x_1^{\mathrm{pred}}[3{:}5]$, are sent as a velocity setpoint $v_{\mathrm{cmd}}$ to PX4's internal velocity loop, which the MPC's own dynamics model ($\dot v=u$, i.e. an assumed zero-lag, instantaneous realization) does not represent. This interface — where the OCP's assumed dynamics and the plant's actual low-level dynamics diverge — is the locus of the diagnosis in §5. **Fig. 1 (generated, `report/paper_figures/fig1_interface_block.png`):** block diagram, OCP → velocity setpoint → [PX4 velocity loop, first-order lag $\tau$] → realized velocity → state feedback, annotating this interface as the injection point of the mismatch.
>
> **Why PX4 SITL is the appropriate testbed for this particular question.** Because the object of study is a *mismatch between two models of the same interface*, it is worth stating explicitly what a software-in-the-loop environment does and does not stand in for. PX4 SITL and a hardware build are compiled from one and the same flight-control source tree: the velocity loop $\mathcal{G}$ of Fig. 1 — its proportional gain `MPC_XY_VEL_P_ACC`, its output saturation, and the attitude/rate/allocation cascade beneath it — is the identical code, running at the identical rates, in both. We state the version boundary precisely rather than gloss it: the experiments reported here run PX4 v1.14 in SITL, whereas the airframes we target run v1.16. The difference is a hardware constraint rather than a choice — board support for the target flight controller entered PX4 upstream only at v1.16, so no v1.14 build exists for it. The interface under study is present and structurally unchanged across the two — in particular the governing gain `MPC_XY_VEL_P_ACC` sits at its PX4 default of 1.8 on both, which we verified directly on the target vehicles' flight controllers — and every claim we make is about that structure rather than about a version-specific numerical value. What the simulator substitutes is the *plant* around which that firmware closes its loops: rigid-body aerodynamics, motor and ESC dynamics, and sensor noise. The distinction matters here because the two halves of the mismatch fall on opposite sides of it. The half we do claim is that the planner's internal model asserts $\mathcal{G}=\mathrm{I}$ while $\mathcal{G}$ is in fact a lag with a saturation downstream of it — a *structural* property of the firmware and of the OCP, both of which SITL reproduces exactly. The half we do **not** claim from SITL is the numerical value of $\tau$ on any given airframe, since the velocity loop closes around the substituted plant; a different vehicle, payload or firmware tuning will identify a different $\tau$. This distinction is not a hedge but the paper's own result: §6.2d shows the failure is governed by the *structure* of the mismatch and not by its *magnitude*, because a model asserting $\tau=0$ is wrong by 100 % whatever the true lag happens to be. Two further caveats belong with it: the wind used throughout is a controlled constant lateral force applied in Gazebo rather than turbulence, and no aerodynamic or sensor-degradation effect of real flight is represented. The remaining limitations of SITL-only evidence are stated in §7.2.

## 5. Diagnosis: From Misattribution to Root Cause

这是全文重心，直接对应报告 §5.G 的溯源叙事，但要**去掉时间线味道**，改写成"假设-检验"的科学论证结构：

### 5.1 现象

**正文草稿：**

> Circular-trajectory formation scenarios exhibited intermittent tracking degradation flagged as REVIEW by our SITL acceptance gates, and under a physics-level wind disturbance (a constant lateral force applied in Gazebo, calibrated to $\approx$4–8 m/s equivalent wind), the same formations exhibited a growing oscillation reaching a wind-forced peak-to-peak positional error of 33.14 m. **Fig. 2 (generated, `report/paper_figures/fig2_phenomenon.png`):** phenomenon data — trio3-circle unforced tracking-error time series and cross5-circle wind-forced divergence, drawn from `flight_trio3_circle.csv` and `flight_W1/W2/W3_cross5_circle_*.csv` via `report/make_paper_figures.py`.

### 5.2 假设 1（证伪）：算法属性

**正文草稿：**

> Our first hypothesis was that the oscillation was an artifact of the control law itself. An outer, undamped proportional correction term had been layered on top of the MPC's own predicted velocity output; removing it stabilized the previously oscillatory unforced circular case. However, the oscillation reappeared under wind disturbance even with the outer loop removed, falsifying "algorithmic property" as a complete explanation — the outer loop had only been masking a deeper cause.

### 5.3 假设 2（证伪，否定性结果）：外部扰动

**正文草稿：**

> Our second hypothesis was that an unmodelled exogenous disturbance (wind) was responsible, motivating an online disturbance-observer construction as a candidate remedy. > This negative result should not be read merely as "our particular observer under-performed." As argued in §3.3, disturbance observers of the FxTDO family estimate an *additive force term in the state equation* ($\dot v = \ldots + \hat f_d/m$) under a quasi-static (slowly-varying) assumption; the true root cause identified next (§5.4) is a mismatch in the *input channel* itself ($\dot v = (v_{\mathrm{sp}}-v)/\tau$ vs. the MPC's assumed $\dot v = u$). The estimate collapses toward zero not because the mismatch is absent, but because its steady-state component is cancelled by the velocity loop's integral action while its dynamic component — a state-dependent, fast-varying equivalent disturbance — violates the quasi-static assumption on which FxTDO-style compensation rests (§3.3). We therefore read this as evidence that DOB-MPC of this family is *ill-suited* to an input-channel lag — not as proof that no observer whatsoever could help — and specify a direct closed-loop comparison (§6.5) to confirm it empirically rather than by argument alone.

### 5.4 真根因：接口失配

**正文草稿：**

> We identified the true root cause by bypassing the MPC and comparing PX4's commanded and realized velocity directly at 130 Hz (5 vehicles, degraded frames excluded; analysis released as `report/tau_goodness.py`). In still air the velocity loop is well fit by a first-order lag $\dot v=(v_{\mathrm{cmd}}-v)/\tau$: the identified model reconstructs the realized velocity almost exactly (forward-integration reconstruction $R^2=0.999$, RMSE $\approx0.02$ m/s over the pre-wind window), with the adopted $\tau=0.48$ s at the low end of a free-fit spread of $0.49$–$0.65$ s (median 0.55 s, s.d. 0.07 s across fit windows and axes) — all consistent with PX4's own documented tracking-delay range of 0.1–0.5 s (§2). Under wind we deliberately do *not* re-identify $\tau$: the vehicle is then in the very saturation-driven divergence under study, so the commanded-vs-realized relationship is dominated by that divergence rather than the clean velocity loop, and a free first-order fit becomes ill-conditioned (across windows the free-fit $\tau$ swings from negative to several seconds with ODE-residual $R^2\approx0$). The adopted still-air $\tau=0.48$ s nonetheless reconstructs the realized velocity even under wind to $R^2\ge0.96$ (RMSE 0.21 m/s) — adequate for the fix, which §6.2b shows is insensitive to $\tau$ over $[0.4,0.8]$ s. We therefore report a single still-air $\tau$ and treat the earlier impression that $\tau$ "lengthens" under wind as an identification artifact of the divergence, not a change in the physical loop constant (§7.2). The MPC's internal model assumes $\dot v = u$ ($\tau=0$); against the identified plant, this causes the MPC to overestimate its own achievable acceleration by roughly an order of magnitude (nominal OCP bound $a_{\max}=4$ m/s², realized 0.13–0.53 m/s² under the identified lag). Because the MPC repeatedly plans a physically unrealizable recovery action, any perturbation that pushes the commanded-vs-realized velocity gap past the velocity-command saturation threshold ($\approx2$ m of accumulated position error) triggers a self-sustained oscillation whose amplitude and period scale with the velocity limit `max_speed` — a scaling we confirmed directly by comparing two otherwise-identical runs at `max_speed`=3.0 and 5.0 m/s. PX4's own controller documentation [PX4Docs] describes the single-vehicle ingredients directly: position control is a proportional controller acting on position error whose velocity command is then saturated by an explicit velocity limit — so that, absent that limit, a growing position error would command velocities the lagging vehicle cannot realize — which is precisely the bounded single-agent seed of the divergence studied here (§5.5). **A terminological clarification we adopt throughout:** in the *unforced* circular case this behaviour is a genuine saturation-bounded limit cycle (a closed orbit in the error/error-rate phase plane, Fig. 4); under *sustained wind forcing* it is no longer bounded — because the persistently moving reference keeps re-injecting error faster than the saturated command can null it, the position error grows without a fixed bound (individual runs reaching hundreds of metres, §6.2b). We therefore describe the unforced case as a limit cycle and the wind-forced case as a saturation-triggered *divergence*, and avoid using "limit cycle" for the latter; where a single term is needed for both regimes we write "saturation-driven instability." We stress that this is a *parasitic* limit cycle — an unwanted failure mode of the closed loop — not one of the *intentional* limit-cycle references sometimes designed into swarm and formation controllers for circling or reactive obstacle avoidance. **Fig. 4（两半均已生成）**：相图半张 `fig4_limit_cycle_partial.png`（跟踪误差 vs 误差变化率，`max_speed`=3.0 与 5.0 m/s 两条对比，直接印证"幅值/周期随限幅缩放"，本地数据）；τ 一阶拟合半张 `fig4b_tau_fit.png`（子图曲线展示 drone 0，commanded/realized 速度叠图 + 一阶模型重建）。**图中的一阶重建用的是全篇采纳的辨识值 $\tau=0.48$ s（报告 §5.G），与正文一致；独立 5 机 pooled 最小二乘作为交叉核对，$\hat\tau$ 随拟合窗口/轴在 $\approx0.49$–$0.65$ s 散布（中位 0.55 s），0.48 s 落在其低端。这一窗口敏感的散布本身就是 §7.2(ii) 的"一阶模型不完整"局限，且由 §6.2b 的 τ-不敏感结果（修复在 $[0.4,0.8]$ s 均有效）可知对结论无影响——因此正文全程采用 0.48 s。**

### 5.5 假设3（证伪）：队形耦合是否为放大器

**正文草稿：**

> The interface mismatch above already destabilizes a *single* vehicle: under wind, a lone agent running the nominal (zero-lag) model does not merely lose tracking accuracy but enters a saturation-driven divergence (solo1, wind-forced peak-to-peak error median **49 m**, §6.2c). The single-vehicle lag is therefore the *seed* of the instability, not a benign effect that coupling alone renders unstable. Our third hypothesis — the one we most expected to hold — was that coupling agents through a *persistent* formation-keeping cost (§4) *amplifies* this already-divergent single-vehicle instability across the swarm: the formation term continuously pulls each agent toward its neighbours' (lag-degraded) predicted or current positions, so a swarm should fare worse than a lone vehicle. The divergence does indeed roughly double in a five-vehicle formation. But the controlled decomposition below (§6.2c) does **not** support the mechanism: removing the formation-keeping weight changes nothing measurable, removing the collision term as well changes nothing measurable, and the doubling survives in full with *every* explicit inter-vehicle coupling switched off. We therefore report this hypothesis as falsified — the third of three — and make no claim that formation coupling is the amplifier.
>
> As argued in §3.2, this is not simply a case the prior literature happened not to test. [KamelIROS17] combine the same lag-identification-and-augmentation technique with a *reactive* coupling scheme — a pairwise avoidance constraint, active only near a predicted conflict and based on a constant-velocity extrapolation of the neighbour's state — which imposes no persistent, always-active bias toward a *shared* reference. We are careful not to over-claim here: our own decomposition (§6.2c) falsifies the mechanism we had expected — the multi-vehicle increase survives with the formation weight switched off, and then with the collision term switched off as well — so we do not claim the reactive/persistent distinction, or any other coupling ingredient, as the operative one. The contrast with [KamelIROS17] is therefore about *architecture*, not mechanism. [Erunsal19] combine true formation keeping — a persistent bias toward a fixed relative-position reference, structurally identical in kind to ours — with an unidentified nominal dynamics model, so the interface mismatch that would drive the interaction is simply absent from their model. The architecture in which we observe the failure combines *both* ingredients (Table I): a persistent, always-active coupling bias, and a state-augmented model whose assumed lag can desynchronize from the plant's true lag. To our knowledge this combination has not previously been instantiated. We record that as the architectural setting in which we encountered the failure, and explicitly *not* as an account of why it has gone unreported — §6.2c shows the divergence arises on a *single* vehicle carrying no formation cost at all, so neither ingredient of Table I is necessary for it, and why it has not been reported before remains an open question. We further isolate the second ingredient: a memoryless consensus law carrying the *same* persistent formation coupling but *no* internal prediction model does not exhibit the limit cycle even when its gain is raised until it saturates more deeply than the diverging MPC (§6.5). The instability therefore requires not merely the lagged plant, but a *predictive* controller whose internal model is blind to that lag — sharpening the second ingredient of Table I from "a lag is identified and augmented" to "a receding-horizon model is *wrong* about the lag." **Fig. 5 (generated, `report/paper_figures/fig5_propagation.png`):** multi-vehicle track-error time series (5 drones, wind-forced, legacy model, from `flight_exp4_A_legacy.csv`) showing the correlated cross-vehicle divergence described above.

**A controlled decomposition of the multi-vehicle increase (completed; see §6.2c for the data):**

> To separate the single-vehicle *seed* from any multi-vehicle *amplification*, we ran — under the identical legacy ($\tau_{\mathrm{model}}=0$), wind-forced protocol, $n=5$, medians reported given the strong right-skew of §6.2b — a single vehicle and a formation-weight sweep (data and figures in §6.2c). **(i) Single-vehicle seed:** solo1 diverges to a wind-window peak-to-peak of median 49 m (range 45–55), and the interface-consistent fix collapses it to 0.24 m — establishing that the mechanism, and its remedy, already operate on one vehicle. **(ii) Implicit coupling:** five vehicles with the formation weight set to zero ($w_f=0$) — still sharing a common leader reference, world frame, and collision term — diverge to a worst-agent median of 125 m (per-agent median 102 m) — roughly 2.1$\times$ the single-vehicle seed on the per-agent statistic, which §6.2c shows is the one comparable across fleet sizes — so a substantial part of the amplification is carried by these implicit couplings *even with the explicit formation cost switched off*. **(iii) Explicit formation weight:** restoring and increasing $w_f$ (0.25 / 0.5 / 1.0 / 2.0) moves the *worst-agent* median to 112 / 153 / 169 / 158 m but the *typical* agent barely at all (102 $\to$ 110 m across an eight-fold weight change, and non-monotonically); a matched $n=5$ test of $w_f$ on vs. off gives $p=0.55$ (§6.2c). **(iv) No explicit coupling at all:** removing the formation weight *and* the collision term together ($w_f=w_c=0$) leaves the divergence essentially unchanged on both statistics — worst-agent median 131 m (vs. 125 m at $w_f=0$), per-agent median 96.9 m (vs. 102 m) — and still significantly above the single vehicle ($p=0.008$, per-agent), which falsifies the hypothesis outright. We therefore state the claim only in its supported form: *the single-vehicle velocity-loop lag already diverges under wind; a five-vehicle simulation roughly doubles it, but no explicit coupling term accounts for that increase and we leave its origin open (§6.2c); and the interface-consistent fix eliminates the divergence at every level, from one vehicle (49$\to$0.24 m) to the full formation (§6.2).* This corrects our initial framing (that a benign single-vehicle lag was rendered unstable only by the formation cost) and, by anchoring the fix at the single-vehicle level, strengthens rather than weakens its generality.

### 5.6 A design condition and a stability certificate（**2026-07-16 新增，理论化尝试——不是新实验，纯粹对已有数据/已知OCP权重做小信号分析**）

> **正文草稿（拟稿，供你审）：**
>
> The preceding sections establish empirically *that* the velocity-loop lag destabilizes the loop, and that the instability is not removed by low-level retuning. We now ask *when* — for what combination of identified lag, formation weight, and coupling topology — the interface-consistent controller of §6.1 retains a stability margin, using only the MPC's own cost weights (§4) and standard linear systems tools, without new experiments. This yields a *design condition*, and we offer it as nothing more: §6.2c's falsification of the coupling hypothesis, and the controlled sweep reported at the end of this section — which does not recover the ordering the condition implies — together mean it is not a validated explanation of the observed severities.
>
> **Linearized closed-loop model.** Away from the (hinge-activated, near-collision-only) collision term, each agent's steady-state MPC feedback for a double-integrator tracking cost $q_p e^2 + q_v\dot e^2 + r_a u^2$ is the infinite-horizon LQR gain $u = -k_p e - k_v\dot e$ with $k_p=\sqrt{q_p/r_a}$, $k_v=\sqrt{(2\sqrt{r_aq_p}+q_v)/r_a}$; with this system's production weights ($q_p=4.0$, $q_v=2.0$, $r_a=0.1$) this gives $k_p\approx6.32$ s$^{-2}$, $k_v\approx5.71$ s$^{-1}$. Linearizing the persistent formation cost $w_f\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}(d_{ij}-d_{ij}^\star)^2$ around the nominal rigid formation shape and restricting to the along-baseline error component (the direction in which the cost is stiffest) reduces the coupling, for each spatial mode of the formation's graph Laplacian $L$ (eigenvalue $\lambda\ge0$; $\lambda=0$ is the common leader-following mode, unaffected by formation coupling), to an added stiffness term $w_f\lambda$ on top of $k_p$. Combining this feedback with the plant's identified first-order lag $\dot v=(v_{\mathrm{sp}}-v)/\tau$ yields, per mode, a standard damped second-order error dynamic $\ddot e + \frac{1+k_v}{\tau}\dot e + \frac{k_p+w_f\lambda}{\tau}e = 0$, with damping ratio
> $$\zeta(\tau,\lambda) = \frac{1+k_v}{2\sqrt{\tau\,(k_p+w_f\lambda)}}.$$
>
> **What this does and does not explain.** For every $\tau,\lambda\ge0$ this system is linearly stable (both coefficients stay positive), so this small-signal model — like any linear analysis of this loop — cannot by itself reproduce the large-amplitude limit cycle of §5.1/§6.2; that requires the velocity-saturation nonlinearity of §5.4, which sets the oscillation's amplitude scale — while its *driver*, the predictive controller's over-commanding against a lag-blind internal model, we make precise in the robustness view below. What $\zeta(\tau,\lambda)$ does provide is a closed-form **stability margin**: it decreases monotonically in both $\tau$ (the identified lag) and $\lambda$ (coupling strength), predicting that more tightly coupled topologies sit closer to the critically-damped edge ($\zeta\to1$) at a fixed identified lag, and are therefore expected to require a smaller triggering disturbance and to ring longer once saturation is engaged — even though, at this system's identified $\tau\approx0.48$–$0.65$ s and production $w_f=0.5$, none of the four graphs below is predicted to cross $\zeta=1$ on its own.
>
> **Nominal stability of the interface-consistent fix (Proposition).** The interface-consistent controller (§6.1) adopts the *correct* velocity model $\dot v=(v_{\mathrm{sp}}-v)/\tau$, so the per-mode error dynamics above *are* its closed loop. Its characteristic polynomial is $s^2+\tfrac{1+k_v}{\tau}\,s+\tfrac{k_p+w_f\lambda}{\tau}$. Because $\tau>0$, $1+k_v>0$, and $k_p+w_f\lambda>0$ for every $\tau>0$ and $\lambda\ge0$, both coefficients are strictly positive; for a second-order polynomial this is the necessary and sufficient Routh–Hurwitz condition for asymptotic stability. **The fix is therefore nominally asymptotically stable at every formation topology and every identified lag**, with the guaranteed margin quantified by $\zeta(\tau,\lambda)$. This is a statement about the *fixed* loop; the legacy loop, whose internal model is wrong, is analysed in the robustness view below.

> **Absolute stability under velocity-command saturation (Proposition 2).** The proposition above is a *linear* statement, and the failure mode of §5.4 is driven by the velocity-command saturation — so a stability claim that ignores that nonlinearity leaves the decisive element unaddressed. We therefore strengthen it. Treat the saturation as a memoryless nonlinearity in the sector $[0,1]$ (which $\mathrm{sat}(\cdot)$ satisfies, since $\mathrm{sat}(u)/u\in(0,1]$), and place it at the controller output. Per Laplacian mode the loop transfer of the interface-consistent controller is
> $$L(s)=\frac{K_1+K_2 s}{s(\tau s+1)},\qquad K_1=k_p+w_f\lambda,\quad K_2=k_v,$$
> whose real part evaluates in closed form to
> $$\operatorname{Re}L(j\omega)=\frac{K_2-K_1\tau}{1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}}.$$
> The circle criterion for a sector-$[0,1]$ nonlinearity requires $\operatorname{Re}L(j\omega)>-1$ for all $\omega$. The expression above is monotone in $\omega^{2}$, taking its extreme value at $\omega=0$ and tending to $0$ as $\omega\to\infty$, so its infimum over $\omega$ is $\min\{K_2-K_1\tau,\,0\}$ and the requirement collapses to $K_2-K_1\tau>-1$ — a single closed-form condition on the design constants:
> $$\boxed{\ \tau\,(k_p+w_f\lambda)\;<\;k_v+1\ }$$
> **When it holds, the interface-consistent loop is absolutely stable for the velocity-command saturation — no sustained oscillation of any amplitude is possible in this per-mode model.** Substituting this system's constants ($k_p=6.32$, $k_v=5.71$, $w_f=0.5$) gives an admissible plant lag of $\tau<1.06$ s for an isolated vehicle and $0.86/0.83/0.76/0.72$ s for `trio3`/`star5`/`cross5`/`grid9` respectively — so the identified $\tau\approx0.48$ s sits inside the certificate with a factor $1.5$ (densest graph) to $2.2$ (isolated) of margin. (A note on the criterion's hypotheses: $L(s)$ is not Hurwitz — it carries the integrator of the velocity-to-position channel — so we invoke the circle criterion in its Nyquist form rather than the textbook stable-plant form. $L$ has no open-right-half-plane poles, and on the indented Nyquist contour the pole at the origin maps to a large arc in the *right* half-plane, which therefore neither enters nor encircles the forbidden half-plane $\operatorname{Re}\le-1$; the boxed condition is exactly the requirement that the remaining locus stay clear of it.) The condition also states the design trade-off explicitly: admissible lag shrinks as coupling density $\lambda$ or formation weight $w_f$ grows, and for a heterogeneous fleet it is the *largest* per-vehicle $\tau$ that binds (§7.2).
>
> Three honesty notes accompany this result. (i) It is *sufficient, not necessary*, and conservative: simulating the same per-mode loop with saturation, we find convergence for $\tau$ well beyond the certified bound (to $\ge2.4$ s), so violating the condition does not imply instability. (ii) It certifies the *static-equivalent* loop of the interface-consistent controller under the simplifying assumptions (i)--(iii) below, not the receding-horizon controller with its full constraint set; a guarantee for the latter would require terminal ingredients (a terminal cost that is a control-Lyapunov function plus a terminal set), which this implementation does not have — we note it as future work rather than claim it. (iii) Because the legacy and interface-consistent controllers share the same static-equivalent gains, the criterion certifies *both*, while only the former diverges in practice. That is not a defect of the criterion but a restatement of this paper's thesis: the legacy failure is not a sector-nonlinearity phenomenon that absolute-stability tools can see, but an over-commanding of the *predictive* internal model, for which the causal evidence remains the non-predictive consensus baseline of §6.5.
>
> **A concrete, falsifiable topology ordering.** Using the exact neighbour graphs already defined for this system's `trio3`, `star5`, `cross5`, and `grid9` scenarios (§4, `config/scenarios.yaml`), the formation-Laplacian's largest eigenvalue is $\lambda_{\max}=3$ (trio3, complete graph $K_3$), $3.618$ (star5, 5-cycle ring), $5$ (cross5, hub-and-spoke star $K_{1,4}$), and $6$ (grid9, $3\times3$ grid graph $P_3\square P_3$) — a strict ordering trio3 $<$ star5 $<$ cross5 $<$ grid9. Substituting into $\zeta(\tau,\lambda_{\max})$ at $\tau=0.48$ s gives $\zeta\approx1.93$ (isolated agent, $\lambda=0$), $1.73$ (trio3), $1.70$ (star5), $1.63$ (cross5), $1.59$ (grid9): a monotonically shrinking margin in the same order. **This predicts that, under an equal disturbance, grid9 should exhibit the most severe/persistent oscillation, followed by cross5, star5, and trio3 least** — a concrete, quantitative and falsifiable *ordering*, which we test against a controlled matched-protocol sweep below — and which, we report in advance, that sweep does not bear out. Two caveats must be read with that comparison, and we state them before the data rather than after: (i) $\zeta$ characterizes the *interface-consistent* loop (simplifying assumption (iii) below), whereas the sweep is necessarily run on the *legacy* loop, where the divergence is large enough to measure — so the sweep tests the predicted ordering, not the predicted margin of the loop it was derived for; and (ii) across these four graphs $\lambda_{\max}$ co-varies with fleet size (3, 5, 5 and 9 vehicles), so only the `star5`-vs-`cross5` pair holds vehicle count fixed.
>
> **Relation to delay-margin theory.** That a networked agreement system's stability margin is governed by the graph Laplacian's spectrum is classical: for consensus dynamics with a single communication delay, the delay margin is set by one "responsible" Laplacian eigenvalue [SipahiQiao11], and recent analyses give it explicitly via the Lambert-$W$ function [Badran25]. Our $\zeta(\tau,\lambda)$ is a specialization of this delay-margin-versus-eigenvalue relationship to a *receding-horizon formation-MPC* setting: the "delay" is the identified velocity-loop lag $\tau$ rather than a communication delay, and the coupling enters as an added cost stiffness $w_f\lambda$ on top of the MPC's own feedback gains rather than through a raw agreement protocol. Reading the ordering above as an instance of this known structural law — not an isolated observation — both grounds it and delimits it: like those results it is a *linear* margin, necessary but not sufficient for the saturation-driven large-signal instability (§5.4) whose amplitude it does not set. The lineage is theoretical; it does not carry empirical weight here, since the sweep below does not recover the ordering the margin implies.
>
> **A robustness view, and why the divergence is *not* a describing-function limit cycle.** The legacy controller designs its feedback as if velocity were realized instantaneously — i.e. against the nominal velocity-to-position plant $P_0(s)=1/s$ — whereas the true plant is $P(s)=\tfrac{1}{s(\tau s+1)}$. The unmodelled dynamics are the multiplicative model error $\Delta(s)=\tfrac{P(s)}{P_0(s)}-1=\tfrac{1}{\tau s+1}-1=\tfrac{-\tau s}{\tau s+1}$, whose magnitude $|\Delta(j\omega)|=\tfrac{\tau\omega}{\sqrt{1+\tau^2\omega^2}}$ rises monotonically from $0$ at DC to $1$ at high frequency. By the small-gain robust-stability condition, a loop tuned on $P_0$ remains stable on $P$ only while $|\Delta(j\omega)\,T_0(j\omega)|<1$, with $T_0$ the nominal complementary sensitivity; since $|\Delta|\to1$, the surviving margin is set by the closed-loop bandwidth, and the more aggressively the MPC commands — the closer it drives the velocity command toward saturation — the higher that bandwidth and the smaller the margin. This makes precise the qualitative statement of §5.4 that *approaching saturation* is what erodes stability. **A describing-function reading of the same algebra agrees, and is reported only as a consistency check** — harmonic balance is an approximation, so the rigorous statement is Proposition 2 above, not the argument that follows. Closing the loop through the velocity-command saturation and applying harmonic balance, the linear part is $G(j\omega)=\dfrac{k_p+k_v\,j\omega}{j\omega\,(1+j\omega\tau)}$; its imaginary part is $\propto-\omega\,(k_p+k_v\tau\omega^2)<0$ for all $\omega>0$ and its real part is $\propto\big(k_v-(k_p+w_f\lambda)\tau\big)=K_2-K_1\tau$ — the same quantity the circle criterion above turns into a certificate — which is positive for every topology considered here ($2.68$ for an isolated vehicle down to $1.24$ for `grid9`, at $\tau=0.48$ s), so $G(j\omega)$ stays in the open fourth quadrant and *never* meets the negative-real critical locus $-1/N(A)$ of a saturation or relay ($N(A)\in(0,1]$ real). No sustained oscillation is predicted by the static-equivalent controller plus saturation. The observed limit cycle is therefore not accounted for by the static equivalent at all. The causal evidence that it is a *predictive* phenomenon comes from the consensus baseline of §6.5 rather than from this argument; the mechanism it points to is that, because the receding-horizon controller's internal model assumes $\dot v=u$, it persistently over-predicts the velocity it will realize and keeps commanding beyond the achievable value until the command saturates — a windup-type mechanism absent from the equivalent LQR gains, whose amplitude consequently scales with the saturation level $v_{\max}$ (Fig. 4) rather than with any describing-function crossing. This is exactly the mechanism the non-predictive consensus baseline of §6.5 isolates empirically: a memoryless law with the *same* equivalent gains but *no* internal over-prediction does not enter the limit cycle even when driven far deeper into saturation — corroborating, from the stability side, that the pathology lives in the predictive controller's (lag-blind) internal model rather than in the coupling, the plant, or the saturation alone.
>
> **Simplifying assumptions (stated explicitly, not swept under the rug).** (i) The linearization drops the collision-avoidance hinge term, valid away from near-collision configurations. (ii) Projecting the formation cost onto a single "along-baseline" scalar mode per Laplacian eigenvalue ignores the direction of each pairwise baseline (a full treatment would use the formation's rigidity matrix rather than the plain graph Laplacian); the plain-Laplacian eigenvalues used here are therefore a first-order proxy for coupling strength, not an exact rigidity-theoretic derivation. (iii) This analysis holds $\tau$ fixed at the plant's *true* identified value — i.e., it characterizes the margin of the already-fixed, interface-consistent controller (§6.1) as a function of topology, not the mechanism behind the much larger, legacy zero-lag-assumption divergence of §5.1/§6.2, which is a distinct internal-model-mismatch effect not modelled here. We report this analysis as a design condition on the fixed controller, not as a complete stability proof, and not as an explanation of the legacy divergence's magnitude.
>
> **Controlled empirical test ($n=5$ per topology, matched protocol) — the predicted ordering is not recovered.** An initial exploratory pass ($n=2$ per topology, each formation left at its own pre-existing leader-circle speed) supported the prediction only coarsely — the sparsest topology (`trio3`) was clearly least affected, but the finer ordering among the denser topologies did not resolve, plausibly because leader speed was not matched across formations. To remove that confound we ran the same legacy ($\tau_{\mathrm{model}}=0$), wind-forced protocol with leader circling speed and recording duration matched across all four topologies (0.5 m/s, 300 s), $n=5$ independent repetitions per topology, and one and the same controller and firmware build throughout. ## 6. Interface-Consistent MPC: Fix & Validation

**正文草稿：**

> **6.1 Method.** Following [Kamel16] and [Stastny18], we fold the identified first-order lag into the OCP's own prediction model, replacing the assumed dynamics $\dot v=u$ with $\dot v=(v_{\mathrm{sp}}-v)/\tau$ and outputting the raw control input $u_0=v_{\mathrm{sp}}$ directly (rather than a one-step-ahead predicted state) as the velocity command, with $\tau\approx0.5$ s from the identification in §5.4. **We do not claim novelty in this technique**; the contribution is the diagnosis that motivates it, and the demonstration that a single, physically identified constant is sufficient — no observer, no retuning, no added inference cost — to remove the failure at every fleet size we tested, from one vehicle to nine (§6.2, §6.2c).
>
> **6.2 SITL validation.** Under the same wind-forcing protocol as §5.1, the fix reduces the wind-forced along-wind peak-to-peak positional error from 33.14 m to 0.13 m and the along-wind standard deviation from 8.28 m to 0.02 m (both are per-vehicle statistics averaged over the five vehicles for $t\ge110$ s, and are reproducible from the released CSVs via `report/headline_provenance.py`), with zero safety violations and zero controller fallbacks (vs. 15 and 106 respectively before the fix). Under the unforced circular-trajectory case (no wind, `safety_filter` active in both conditions), steady-state tracking error falls from 0.368 m to 0.035 m (mean) and from 0.815 m to 0.114 m (max) — moving the scenario from REVIEW to PASS under our 0.5 m acceptance threshold — without retuning any cost weight. A secondary, physically-grounded discrepancy independently flagged during visual inspection (one vehicle appearing higher than another in a three-vehicle circular formation) was traced to the same interface-consistent model correcting an EKF-invisible altitude estimation bias, reducing the Gazebo-ground-truth inter-vehicle height difference from a mean of 1.04 m (peak 1.30 m) to 0.12 m (peak 0.35 m) — an $\approx$8.7$\times$ reduction, confirmed against a physics-layer bypass monitor independent of the vehicles' own state estimators.
>
> **6.2b Repeated-trial and $\tau$-sensitivity results.** The single-run effect sizes in §6.2 were obtained from one trial per condition; we additionally ran $n\ge5$ independent repetitions of the unforced and wind-forced circular scenarios (before/after the fix) and a six-point sweep of the augmented model's assumed $\tau$, to quantify run-to-run variance and confirm the fix's effect is not an artifact of a single favourable trial. Two methodological notes apply to all repeated trials reported here: (i) this SITL stack has no fixed random-number seed governing trial-to-trial variation — variance instead arises from genuine scheduling and EKF-convergence timing nondeterminism across independent Gazebo/PX4/DDS process restarts, so these are repeated real-timing trials rather than a seeded Monte Carlo study; (ii) each repetition restarts the full simulation stack (Gazebo, all PX4 instances, the DDS bridge) rather than only the controller, since the terminal state of one trajectory would otherwise contaminate the next trial's initial condition. **Statistical comparisons.** Because the wind-forced distributions are strongly right-skewed (a saturation-triggered instability whose amplitude depends on trigger timing), all group comparisons in this paper use the exact two-sided Mann–Whitney $U$ test ($n=5$ per group unless noted), reported with the Cliff's $\delta$ effect size and a $2\times10^4$-sample bootstrap 95 % confidence interval on the difference in medians; $p=0.008$ is the smallest value attainable at $n=5{,}5$, so with these sample sizes only order-of-magnitude effects (the fix, the consensus baseline) reach significance, while within-order differences (e.g. DOB vs. legacy) do not — a limitation we state explicitly rather than over-read. Around twenty pairwise comparisons are reported across §5.6, §6.2b, §6.2c, §6.2d and §6.5; we apply no multiplicity correction, and note that the one topology result that does survive — `trio3`, the smallest fleet, below every larger one at $p\le0.004$ — also survives a Bonferroni adjustment for the four topology comparisons, while the DOB and consensus comparisons are read as effect-size statements rather than as a family of tests. The one-sided tests in §5.6 are directional because $\zeta(\tau,\lambda)$ predicts the direction in advance, not because the direction was chosen after inspecting the data; that the direction was fixed beforehand is precisely what makes the sweep's failure to confirm it informative rather than merely inconclusive. The analysis script and the per-run flight logs it recomputes from are released with the code (`report/stats_significance.py`, which recomputes them from the per-run logs under `report/data/`).
>
> Unforced circular tracking (mean steady-state pos. error, $n=5$): before the fix, $0.359\pm0.002$ m; after, $0.035\pm0.001$ m — consistent with the single-run values in §6.2 (0.368 m / 0.035 m) and showing low run-to-run variance in both conditions. Wind-forced tracking under the same $n=5$ protocol shows a starkly different variance profile. *Metric.* Unless noted otherwise, a run's wind-window peak-to-peak error is the largest single-vehicle peak-to-peak position error over the wind window — that run's **worst agent**; §6.2c reports the complementary per-agent statistic and explains when each is appropriate. Pre- and post-fix runs are reduced identically, so every before/after comparison below is like-for-like. Before the fix, wind-window peak-to-peak error has median 153 m and range [119, 816] m across the five runs (individual values: 119, 138, 153, 214, 816 m; mean $288$ m is reported for reference only — with one run at 816 m the distribution is strongly right-skewed and effectively one-sided, so a mean $\pm$ s.d. of $288\pm297$ m over-summarizes it and we rely on the median and range instead). The steady-state error shows the same skew (median 38 m, range [30, 242] m). This extreme run-to-run spread is itself the point: the divergence is not a fixed-amplitude oscillation but a saturation-triggered instability whose magnitude depends on the timing of the triggering perturbation. After the fix, mean steady-state pos. error $0.063\pm0.001$ m and peak-to-peak error $0.612\pm0.022$ m, a two-to-three-order-of-magnitude reduction with markedly tighter variance than the pre-fix condition. The single-run wind-forced values reported in §6.2 (33.14 m / 0.13 m) are **not** directly comparable with these five repetitions: they are along-wind-axis statistics averaged per vehicle, whereas the $n=5$ figures here are peak-to-peak values of the position-error *magnitude* over the wind window. They are therefore reported as a separate, differently-defined effect size rather than as a sample from the same distribution; we report both because the $n=5$ statistics establish that the qualitative result (severe divergence before the fix, tight convergence after) is not a single-trial artifact, while the original single-run values remain the historical reference point used elsewhere in this paper and in the underlying technical report.
>
> The $\tau$-sensitivity sweep (unforced circular scenario, single run per point except the $\tau=0$ and $\tau=0.5$ endpoints which reuse the $n=5$ means above) shows steady-state pos. error falling monotonically as the augmented model's assumed $\tau$ increases from 0 toward the identified value, then plateauing: 0.359 m ($\tau=0$), 0.065 m ($\tau=0.2$), 0.046 m ($\tau=0.3$), 0.036 m ($\tau=0.4$), 0.034 m ($\tau=0.48$, the identified value), 0.035 m ($\tau=0.5$, the production default), 0.035 m ($\tau=0.6$), 0.037 m ($\tau=0.8$). The curve is essentially flat and near-minimal across $\tau\in[0.4,0.8]$ (a 0.003 m spread against a 0.325 m drop over $\tau\in[0,0.4]$), indicating the fix is insensitive to moderate over-estimation of $\tau$ around the true value — a wide enough safety margin that the single first-order fit used in this paper (§5.4), despite being methodologically simpler than the grey-box identification of [Stastny18], is adequate for this application.

> **6.2c Single-vehicle seed, and a falsified coupling hypothesis.** To locate where the divergence originates — and to test the causal role of the formation-keeping weight directly rather than through the topology proxy of §5.6 — we ran two matched controlled experiments under the legacy ($\tau_{\mathrm{model}}=0$), wind-forced protocol of §6.2b ($n=5$; medians reported given the same right-skew). *A single vehicle already diverges:* solo1 reaches a wind-window peak-to-peak of median 49 m (range 45–55 m; steady-state error median 8.7 m), which the interface-consistent fix ($\tau=0.5$) collapses to 0.24 m peak-to-peak (steady-state 0.045 m) — the identical mechanism and remedy as in the formation, operating on one vehicle. *Five vehicles roughly double it, but not through the explicit coupling* (Table II-b): five vehicles with the formation weight set to zero ($w_f=0$) — decoupled in the *explicit* formation cost but still sharing a common leader reference, a common world frame, and a pairwise collision term — diverge to median 125 m, roughly 2.5$\times$ the single-vehicle seed; restoring and increasing the explicit weight ($w_f=0.25/0.5/1.0/2.0$) raises the worst-agent median to 112 / 153 / 169 / 158 m, a further $\approx$1.2–1.4$\times$. **That last figure, however, is metric-dependent and we report it as such.** The two statistics answer different questions: the *worst-agent* value governs formation integrity, while the *per-agent* value (the mean over vehicles of each vehicle's peak-to-peak) is the one that can be compared across fleet sizes without an order-statistic bias — the maximum over five vehicles exceeds the maximum over one even for identical per-vehicle distributions. Under the per-agent statistic the same eight-fold change in $w_f$ (0 $\to$ 2.0) moves the typical vehicle only from 2.1$\times$ to 2.2$\times$ the seed (102 $\to$ 110 m, and non-monotonically: $w_f$=0.25 sits *below* $w_f$=0), whereas the implicit couplings alone already deliver 2.1$\times$. What the explicit formation weight mainly does, therefore, is widen the *spread across vehicles* — which matters operationally, since formation integrity is set by the worst agent — rather than worsen the typical vehicle. Neither explicit coupling term, then, accounts for the amplification. To close the decomposition we removed **both** ($w_f=0$ *and* $w_c=0$, leaving only a shared leader reference and a common world frame): the divergence is essentially unchanged on both statistics (worst-agent median 131 m against 125 m at $w_f=0$; per-agent median 96.9 m against 102 m), and still significantly above the single vehicle ($p=0.008$ on the per-agent statistic, with no overlap between the solo1 range $[45,55]$ m and the decoupled five-vehicle range $[91,130]$ m). A matched single-vehicle comparison sharpens this: `cross5`'s vehicle 0 carries a zero formation offset, so its reference is *pointwise identical* to solo1's, yet with all coupling removed it still diverges to 80.7 m against solo1's 49.0 m ($p=0.008$) — the only difference being that four other vehicles exist in the same simulation. Pre-wind steady-state error for that same vehicle is if anything *lower* in the five-vehicle case (0.144 vs 0.163 m), which argues against a compute-load explanation. We could not determine whether the residual effect stems from the shared reference or from the multi-vehicle simulation environment itself, and we therefore draw no mechanistic conclusion from it. It is, however, reproducible rather than incidental: the topology sweep of §5.6 — a different leader protocol, and three, five and nine vehicles rather than one and five — shows the same monotone growth with fleet size (per-agent wind-window peak-to-peak 77.3, 94.2/130.5 and 155.6 m). That sweep cannot isolate fleet size from coupling density either, since the two co-vary across its graphs; what the two experiments jointly establish is that the effect is real and repeatable across protocols, not that we can explain it. The fix eliminates the divergence at every level (solo1 49$\to$0.24 m; formations §6.2/§6.2b). Data: `report/data/wf_decomp/`.

> **6.2d Is the interface mismatch simply a matter of inner-loop tuning?** A natural objection to the diagnosis is that $\tau\approx0.48$ s reflects an *under-tuned* PX4 velocity loop rather than a property of the interface itself. The objection has a concrete basis: PX4's default horizontal velocity gain is `MPC_XY_VEL_P_ACC`$=1.8$, for which the documented first-order relation gives $\tau\approx1/1.8\approx0.55$ s, and the comparison in §3.3 against [NigusieAST26]'s $\tau=0.03$–$0.08$ s invites exactly that reading. If tuning the inner loop faster removed the failure, the practical implication of §7.3 would be "tune your inner loop", not "model your interface". We therefore tested it directly, under the legacy ($\tau_{\mathrm{model}}=0$), wind-forced `cross5` protocol of §6.2b, raising the PX4 velocity-loop gain to $2\times$ and $3\times$ the default (`MPC_XY_VEL_P_ACC` $=3.6$ and $5.4$, each verified in the per-instance boot log), $n=3$ per setting.

> *Manipulation check — the gain does what it should.* Pre-wind steady-state tracking error falls monotonically with gain: 0.149 m at 1.8, 0.114 m at 3.6, and 0.092 m at 5.4 — a 38 % reduction, significant at both settings ($p=0.036$, the smallest value attainable at $n=3,5$) — while the commanded speed is unchanged (1.016 / 1.014 / 1.012 m/s), i.e. the *same* trajectory is being flown by a measurably faster loop.

We state plainly what this test can and cannot do: at $n=3$ against $n=5$ it would reject only under near-complete separation of the two samples, so it cannot exclude a modest effect, and the substantive claim rests not on these $p$-values but on the magnitudes — across all eleven runs the divergence stays within 78–255 m, i.e. 128–416$\times$ the interface-consistent fix's 0.61 m, so no gain setting brings the legacy controller within two orders of magnitude of the fix.

What matters is the *structural* mismatch, not its magnitude — the same conclusion the fix's $\tau$-insensitivity over $[0.4,0.8]$ s reaches from the other direction (§6.2b). One caveat: we did not re-identify $\tau$ at the new gains, since the 1 Hz telemetry is far too coarse for a $\approx0.2$ s time constant; the quoted $\tau$ values follow PX4's documented $\tau\approx1/$`MPC_XY_VEL_P_ACC` relation, and the manipulation check above is our evidence that the loop did in fact become faster. Rows (c)/(c$'$) are the *second* falsified remedy (§5.3), now measured in closed loop ($n=5$): the FxTDO-style additive observer leaves the wind-forced divergence statistically unchanged at a conservative estimate cap (125 m median vs. legacy 153 m; Mann–Whitney $p=0.15$) and does not significantly change it at an aggressive cap (168 m median; $p=0.55$), confirming the assumption-level prediction of §3.3 that this observer class cannot correct the mismatch — the details and mechanism ($\dot v-u\approx-0.9\,u$, a command-correlated pseudo-disturbance $\Rightarrow$ positive feedback) are given after Table IV. $^\ast$The unforced steady-state column for (c)/(c$'$) is not separately swept: the observer engages only in the mission (wind) phase, so unforced tracking coincides with row (a). Row (e) is analyzed next.

**A non-predictive control baseline (consensus).**

> To test whether the divergence is a generic consequence of coupling a lagged velocity plant through a persistent formation objective — as opposed to something specific to *model-predictive* control — we replace the MPC with a memoryless distributed displacement-consensus law, $v_{\mathrm{cmd},i}=v_{\ell}+k_L\,(p_\ell+\delta_i-p_i)+k_C\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}\big((p_j+\delta_i-\delta_j)-p_i\big)$, driven through the *identical* PX4 velocity loop, wind protocol, and `cross5` scenario as rows (a)/(d). This law retains the persistent, always-active formation bias of §5.5 but carries **no internal prediction model and no receding horizon** — there is nothing for a lag-blind model to mispredict. At its nominal gain ($k_L=1.5$) it stays bounded under wind (wind-window peak-to-peak $0.886\pm0.035$ m, $n=5$; row (e)), two orders of magnitude below legacy MPC (row (a)) with no distributional overlap (Mann–Whitney $p=0.008$, the smallest value attainable at $n=5{,}5$; Cliff's $\delta=-1.0$), and its commanded velocity never saturates (peak $0.93\times$ the speed limit, zero clip events). **This is not merely an artifact of a conservative gain.** Raising the consensus position gain to the MPC's own equivalent LQR value ($k_L=6.3\approx k_p$, §5.6) drives it into velocity saturation roughly four times deeper than legacy MPC (peak command $\approx10.5\times$ the limit; $\approx8.0\times10^4$ clip events per run vs. $\approx1.4\times10^4$ for row (a)) — yet it still does **not** diverge: the wind-forced peak-to-peak error stays bounded at $1.544\pm0.134$ m ($n=5$, again $p=0.008$ vs. legacy, all vehicles remaining within $\le4.9$ m of the origin), albeit holding a loose, oscillatory formation that does not cleanly settle at that gain. Thus even under deeper saturation than the one that destabilizes legacy MPC, the memoryless law does not enter the divergent limit cycle. **We read this as localizing the pathology to the predictive controller's internal lag-blind model** — not to the formation coupling, the plant, or velocity saturation per se — which is consistent with, and mechanistically explains, why the principled remedy corrects that internal model (§6.1) rather than adding an external disturbance term (§3.3, row (c)). **Fig. 7 (generated, `report/paper_figures/fig7_consensus_bounded.png`):** (a) worst-agent tracking error over time under wind — legacy diverges while both consensus gains stay bounded; (b) raising the consensus gain deepens velocity saturation $\approx$11$\times$ (peak commanded speed $0.9\times\to10.7\times$ the limit) while the wind-forced error barely moves ($0.89\to1.54$ m), whereas legacy diverges at only $\approx$2.7$\times$ saturation.

**DOB-MPC baseline (completed) — filling rows (c)/(c$'$) of Table III:**

> We implemented the FxTDO-family observer specified for this falsification and ran it in closed loop. **Observer:** a no-integrator Q-filter disturbance observer (standard Ohishi form, $\hat f_d=\mathrm{LPF}_L[\dot v_{\mathrm{meas}}-u]$ with cutoff $L=1$ rad/s — deliberately low-bandwidth, i.e. quasi-static per [Xu24, Assumption 1]), estimating a lumped translational $\hat f_d\in\mathbb R^3$ from the commanded-vs-realized velocity residual and fed forward into the *same* zero-lag OCP as row (a) via $\dot v=u+\hat f_d$ (added to the legacy model, not to the interface-consistent one); it engages only after formation acquisition so that start-up is identical to legacy. **Protocol:** the identical `cross5`/`trio3` circular wind-forcing of §6.2b, $n=5$, medians reported. **Result — the §3.3 prediction is confirmed:** with a conservative estimate cap (0.5 m/s²) the wind-forced peak-to-peak error is median 125 m (`cross5`, range [0.37, 188] m) / 86.5 m (`trio3`, range [66, 105] m) — statistically indistinguishable from legacy row (a) (153 / 90.7 m; two-sided Mann–Whitney $p=0.15$ for `cross5`, $p=0.42$ for `trio3`) and two orders of magnitude above the interface-consistent fix (0.61 m). The additive observer does not touch the instability. We report the `cross5` range untrimmed: one of those five runs stayed at 0.37 m and never entered the divergence at all. That is the same trigger-timing lottery which gives the legacy condition its [119, 816] m spread, and it is the single most favourable observation the observer receives anywhere in this study — discarding it would only strengthen the negative result, so we keep it. **Raising the observer's authority (cap 2.0 m/s²) does not rescue it either, and the mechanism explains why it cannot.** At the aggressive cap the median is 168 m (`cross5`), but at this variance that is *not* a statistically distinguishable change from legacy (two-sided Mann–Whitney $p=0.55$, Cliff's $\delta=+0.28$, bootstrap 95 % CI on the median difference $[-648,+369]$ m; the aggressive-DOB *mean*, 236 m, is in fact below legacy's 288 m, which a single 816 m legacy run inflates). We therefore do *not* claim the aggressive observer empirically worsens the divergence — at $n=5$ it neither reliably helps nor harms. The informative result is structural: because the velocity loop attenuates the realized acceleration to $\dot v\approx0.1\,u$, the observer's residual is $\dot v-u\approx-0.9\,u$ — a signal *correlated with the command itself*, not an exogenous disturbance. Feeding it back as $\dot v=u+\hat f_d$ therefore acts as positive feedback (loop gain $\sim1/0.1$) that can only push toward, never away from, instability; a tight cap throttles it back toward the legacy response, a loose cap unleashes it. This is the clearest confirmation of N2: an additive DOB estimates the command, not a disturbance, so it is the wrong tool for an input-channel lag at *any* tuning — the point is its structural inapplicability, not an empirical worsening. With this, every row of Table III is built from data already in the repository (`report/data/dob/`); the sole remaining experimental gap is real-flight validation (§7.2).

## 7. Discussion

**正文草稿：**

> **7.1 Why this is a diagnosis, not a method, contribution.** As established in §3.2, augmenting an identified low-level lag into an MPC's prediction model is not new; nor is the observation that PX4's velocity loop lags. The contribution of this paper is showing what happens when a receding-horizon planner is *wrong* about that lag rather than merely subject to it — a saturation-driven instability rather than a tracking penalty, one that low-level retuning does not remove — quantifying it, ruling out the more commonly reached-for explanation (an exogenous disturbance) both empirically and structurally, and validating a fix drawn directly from the existing single-vehicle literature. We also report a hypothesis of our own that we could not sustain: that the persistent formation-keeping cost is what amplifies the mismatch across a swarm (§5.5, §6.2c). We consider this framing more defensible than a "new method" claim, which — as §3.2 shows in detail — would not survive comparison with [Kamel16] or [Stastny18].
>
> **7.2 Limitations.** All validation reported here is SITL-only (PX4 software-in-the-loop with Gazebo physics), and while PX4 SITL is built from the same flight-control source tree as a hardware build — so the *structure* under study is that of a genuine flight-control stack rather than a purely synthetic model (§4), albeit at a different release (v1.14 here against v1.16 on the target airframes; §4) — no real-flight validation has yet been performed, and the *numerical* value $\tau=0.48$ s should be read as specific to this airframe and plant model rather than as a property of PX4 in general. Our own $\tau$-identification procedure is a single first-order fit from one measurement pass, methodologically simpler than the formal grey-box system-identification pipeline of [Stastny18] (§3.2); §6.2b reports a $\tau$-sensitivity sweep and $n\ge5$ repeated-trial statistics under this stack's inherent timing nondeterminism (no fixed random seed governs EKF convergence timing or scheduler jitter), which together show the fix is insensitive to moderate $\tau$ mis-identification and that its effect is not a single-trial artifact — but we have not repeated the $\tau$-identification procedure itself across multiple independent 130 Hz measurement passes to quantify identification-run variance, only the downstream tracking-error effect of assuming different $\tau$ values. Line, hover, and large-perturbation scenarios have not been separately profiled for this specific interaction, though they are covered by the 41-scenario regression of §6.3 for the fix's absence of side effects. A controlled sweep ($n=5$ per topology, matched leader speed, duration, collision weight and build, §5.6) found the sparsest topology significantly least affected but did *not* recover the ordering $\zeta(\tau,\lambda)$ predicts: the two five-vehicle graphs come out transposed, the fleet-size-matched pair resolves in neither direction at $n=5$, and severity instead tracks fleet size — so we report $\zeta$ and Proposition 2 as design conditions rather than as validated predictions (§5.6); an earlier unmatched-speed exploratory pass had not even recovered the trend, underscoring the importance of controlling leader speed across topologies. A direct quantitative comparison against a DOB-MPC baseline under matched conditions has been run (§6.5) and confirms the negative result (additive compensation leaves the divergence statistically intact at any observer tuning). A further gap concerns **heterogeneous lag across vehicles**: every vehicle here runs the same firmware and tuning, so a single fleet-wide $\tau$ is assumed, whereas a realistic formation (mixed airframes, differing payloads or tuning) would present a per-vehicle $\tau$. This is precisely the desynchronization anticipated in §5.5 — a state-augmented model whose lag can drift from the plant's true lag — and it is the most obvious next failure mode for a *formation*; we have not tested it, and an interface-consistent controller carrying the wrong neighbour lag may degrade in ways this study cannot predict. Three further limitations bound the scope of our claims. **(i) Single-system evidence.** All results come from one MPC formulation, one set of cost weights, one solver, and one firmware version (PX4 v1.14); the most direct way to narrow this particular gap needs no hardware at all — repeating the key comparisons in SITL under a second firmware release would test whether the diagnosis survives a version change, and is the more pressing for us because board support for our target flight controller entered PX4 only at v1.16, so the vehicles we intend to fly can never run the version studied here (§4); although we phrase the phenomenon in the general language of distributed formation MPC, our evidence is a single-system case study, and the general claim should be read as a hypothesis supported within — not established beyond — this system. Within this system, however, a non-predictive control baseline (memoryless consensus, §6.5) run through the identical plant and coupling does *not* reproduce the divergence at any gain we tested, which localizes the effect to the predictive controller's internal model rather than to the plant or the coupling; the open generalization question is therefore across MPC formulations, solvers, and weight families (and to real hardware), not whether coupling-plus-lag alone suffices — it does not. The topology sweep (§5.6) and the completed coupling decomposition (§5.5, §6.2c) vary coupling within the system — showing the divergence persists with every explicit inter-vehicle coupling removed, and that a non-predictive consensus law does not reproduce it (§6.5) — but we have not varied the MPC formulation, solver, or weight family. A further limitation is that we could not resolve the origin of the residual five-vehicle-vs-single-vehicle difference (§6.2c): a shared leader reference and the multi-vehicle simulation environment remain confounded, and separating them would require a fleet of vehicles tracking mutually independent references. **(ii) A first-order lag model, characterized only in still air.** The adopted $\tau=0.48$ s is identified from the pre-wind window, where the first-order model reconstructs the realized velocity to $R^2=0.999$ (RMSE $\approx0.02$ m/s) and the free-fit estimate is stable to within $0.49$–$0.65$ s (median 0.55 s, s.d. 0.07 s across windows and axes, §5.4). Under wind $\tau$ is *not* separately identifiable: the diverging dynamics dominate the commanded-vs-realized relationship and the first-order fit becomes ill-conditioned (free-fit $\tau$ ranges from negative to several seconds, ODE-residual $R^2\approx0$), so the earlier impression that $\tau$ "lengthens" under wind is an identification artifact of the divergence rather than a change in the physical loop constant; we accordingly report a still-air $\tau$ only, and note that the adopted value still reconstructs the wind-forced velocity to $R^2\ge0.96$. What we have *not* done is a full grey-box identification in the manner of [Stastny18] (multiple parameters, a train/validation/test split, explicit actuator sub-models) or a repetition across independent measurement passes; the still-air fit, while well-conditioned, remains a single first-order approximation. That the fix is nonetheless effective and $\tau$-insensitive across $[0.4,0.8]$ s (§6.2b) indicates this approximation is adequate for *correcting* the mismatch even where it is imperfect as a *model*. **(iii) SITL-only.** Even the minimal real-flight datapoint that would most raise confidence (e.g. a trio3 hover/line) is absent; SITL runs the same flight-control firmware, but Gazebo physics and the SITL scheduler are not the plant, and we cannot at present exclude that some quantitative details (particularly the magnitude of the wind-forced divergence) are stack-specific.
>
> **7.3 Practical implications.** Designers of distributed formation-MPC systems should verify whether the time constant of the underlying low-level execution interface is implicitly assumed to be zero by the upper-level planner — the check matters even for a single vehicle, since the mismatch is already destabilizing there (§6.2c), and a multi-vehicle deployment roughly doubles the resulting divergence for reasons we could not resolve (§6.2c). This check is inexpensive relative to the failure it can prevent: it requires only a bypass measurement of commanded-vs-realized velocity and, if a lag is found, the state-augmentation technique already established in the single-vehicle literature. It is worth stressing what the check is *not*: retuning the low-level loop is not a substitute. Tripling this stack's velocity-loop gain improves nominal tracking by 38 % yet leaves the wind-forced divergence statistically unchanged (§6.2d), because a planner that assumes zero lag is wrong by 100 % whatever the true lag happens to be.

## 8. Conclusion

**正文草稿：**

> In a single-system SITL case study of a PX4-based distributed MPC swarm (one-to-nine vehicles), we diagnosed a failure mode of the planner/execution interface: a well-known, individually-documented velocity-loop lag in PX4's low-level control stack which, because the planner's model asserts it to be zero, produces not degraded tracking but a saturation-driven instability (a saturation-bounded limit cycle unforced, a divergence under wind) — and one that retuning the low-level loop does not remove. We argued this failure mode to be ill-suited to additive disturbance-observer compensation — and mechanistically distinct from the exogenous disturbances targeted by disturbance-observer-based MPC — and resolved it in SITL by adopting, unmodified, an established single-vehicle lag-augmentation technique, verified across a 41-scenario regression suite and supported by $n\ge5$ repeated-trial statistics and a $\tau$-sensitivity sweep. A direct closed-loop DOB-MPC baseline comparison, a $w_f$-sweep test of the amplification claim, and a matched-protocol topology sweep have since been completed (§6.5, §6.2c, §5.6) — the first confirming that additive compensation leaves the divergence statistically intact at any observer tuning (the residual it estimates is the command, not a disturbance); the second locating the divergence's single-vehicle seed and falsifying our own coupling hypothesis; the third declining to recover the severity ordering our design condition implies, with severity tracking fleet size instead. We report both negative results as they stand: the diagnosis and the fix rest on the controlled before/after comparisons, not on either of them. Whether the mechanism and its remedy transfer across MPC formulations, solvers, and weight families — and, above all, to real hardware — remains a conjecture that this single-system study supports but does not establish; a minimal real-flight datapoint is the principal remaining item of future work.
