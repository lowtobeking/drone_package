# Interface Mismatch, Not Disturbance: Diagnosing and Fixing a Velocity-Loop Lag in PX4 Offboard MPC


## Abstract

Distributed model predictive control (MPC) for multi-UAV formation flight typically assumes each vehicle's velocity controller realizes commanded setpoints instantaneously. We report a case study in which this assumption — documented in PX4 and previously addressed for single vehicles — produces a failure mode — unreported, to our knowledge — when the planner's model asserts the lag away: not degraded tracking, but a saturation-driven instability. In software-in-the-loop (SITL) regression of a one-to-nine-vehicle acados MPC swarm on PX4 v1.14, wind-forced flight diverged, surviving two remedies: removing an outer proportional loop, and an online disturbance observer that recovered only 0.017 m/s$^2$ of a 1.0 m/s$^2$ injection — an input-channel lag being ill-suited to additive compensation. Offline 130 Hz identification revealed a first-order velocity-loop lag $\tau\approx0.48$ s (still-air velocity reconstruction $R^2=0.999$), within PX4's documented 0.1–0.5 s delay; the zero-lag model overestimates its acceleration authority tenfold. Nor is it low-level tuning: tripling the velocity-loop gain improves nominal tracking by 38 % but leaves the divergence statistically unchanged. We also falsify our own hypothesis that the formation-keeping cost amplifies the mismatch: the divergence persists with all explicit coupling removed ($p=0.008$ against one vehicle). Folding the identified lag into the prediction model eliminates the instability (wind-forced peak-to-peak error reduced two-to-three orders of magnitude; unforced tracking error $0.368\to0.035$ m) and passes a 41-scenario regression suite. A quadratic Lyapunov function certifies the corrected loop against velocity-command saturation, with damping margin and disturbance gain invariant to the plant lag. Fix validation is SITL-only; single-vehicle hardware flights add deployability evidence.

## 2. Introduction



Distributed model predictive control (MPC) has become a standard approach to multi-UAV formation flight: each vehicle solves a local optimal control problem and couples to its neighbours through broadcast state, trajectory, or barrier-function information [LiuSurvey24], [XuAccess23], [Goarin24]. PX4 has emerged as a de facto standard flight-control stack for carrying such architectures from simulation to hardware. This paper concerns a distributed acados-based MPC swarm, built on this common template, that we validated through software-in-the-loop (SITL) regression across formations of one to nine vehicles. We present it deliberately as a **single-system case study**: the phenomenon is described in the general language of distributed formation MPC, but all evidence — and therefore every quantitative claim — comes from this one PX4-based stack (one MPC formulation, one solver, one weight set, one firmware version); the controlled before/after comparisons that carry the argument are all in SITL, with real flight contributing a single-vehicle deployability datapoint only (§6.6). We are correspondingly careful to separate what is *demonstrated here* from what is *conjectured to generalize*, and state the boundary explicitly in §7.2.


A common simplifying assumption in these formulations is that the low-level velocity controller realizes a commanded setpoint instantaneously. This assumption is imperfect, and the imperfection is not new information: PX4's own tuning documentation states that realized velocity lags the setpoint, with the lag increasing with the `MPC_ACC_HOR` parameter, and its collision-prevention module budgets a tracking delay in the range of 0.1–0.5 s [PX4Docs]. For single vehicles, identifying this lag from flight data and augmenting it into the MPC's internal prediction model is an established technique: [Kamel16] identify a first-order closed-loop attitude response and augment it into an NMPC state vector on a real hexacopter; [Stastny18] identify a full closed-loop/open-loop grey-box model — including an explicit throttle-lag time constant — for a fixed-wing NMPC guidance law, again validated on real hardware. **We do not claim novelty in this augmentation technique**; we adopt it.


The works above are single-vehicle. In this paper we report what that mismatch does when the planner is a formation MPC whose internal model asserts the lag away: not degraded tracking, but a saturation-driven instability (a limit cycle unforced, a divergence under wind) that is already present on a single vehicle, is amplified in a five-vehicle simulation — an amplification a zero-coupling null model reproduces in full, marking it a scenario rather than a coupling effect (§6.2c) — and is not removed by retuning the low-level loop. We observed this while performing routine SITL regression on a 5-to-9-vehicle formation; our first hypothesis — that the observed oscillation was an artifact of an outer control loop — was falsified by removing that loop, after which the oscillation persisted under wind disturbance. Our second hypothesis — that an unmodelled exogenous disturbance was responsible — was falsified by constructing and testing an online disturbance observer, which recovered only 1.7 % of an injected disturbance's magnitude. We show that this negative result is not an artifact of a poorly tuned observer but a consequence of the true root cause: the mismatch lies in the mapping from the MPC's velocity command to the realized velocity (an input-channel dynamic), whose equivalent disturbance is state-dependent and fast — violating the quasi-static-disturbance assumption on which additive DOB-MPC compensation of the FxTDO family relies, so that such compensation is ill-suited to it regardless of how accurately the (largely DC-cancelled) residual is estimated. This distinguishes the failure mode from the exogenous disturbances addressed by disturbance-observer-based MPC (DOB-MPC) literature such as [Xu24]; a direct closed-loop DOB-MPC comparison confirms it in §6.5 — an additive observer leaves the divergence statistically intact at any tuning, because the quantity it estimates is the command, not a disturbance.


The contributions of this paper are:
1. **Diagnosis.** We identify, formalize, and quantify — to our knowledge for the first time in distributed formation MPC — a failure mode in which a well-documented single-vehicle velocity-loop lag, asserted to be zero by the planner's internal model, produces not degraded tracking but a saturation-driven instability (a saturation-bounded limit cycle without wind, and a large-amplitude divergence under wind), and one that retuning the low-level velocity loop does not remove (§6.2d). The failure is absent from prior work, which either pairs the same lag-identification technique with a *reactive*, non-formation coupling scheme [KamelIROS17] or pairs a formation-keeping cost with an *unidentified* nominal model [Erunsal19].
2. **Negative result.** We show — empirically (an online observer recovers only 0.017 m/s² of a 1.0 m/s² injected disturbance; a matched closed-loop DOB-MPC comparison leaves the wind-forced divergence statistically unchanged at both a conservative and an aggressive observer cap, $n=5$, §6.5) and by an assumption-level argument (an input-channel lag produces a state-dependent, fast equivalent disturbance that violates the quasi-static assumption of additive compensation, while its DC part is already cancelled by the velocity loop's integral action, §3.3) — that this failure is ill-suited to additive disturbance-observer compensation and is mechanistically distinct from the exogenous disturbances such methods target.
3. **Fix and validation.** We fold the identified lag into the OCP's own prediction model — an established single-vehicle technique, which we adopt unmodified — and validate it at a scale not previously reported for it, one to nine vehicles across 41 scenarios: wind-forced peak-to-peak tracking error falls from a median of 153 m to $0.612\pm0.022$ m and unforced circular tracking error from $0.359\pm0.002$ m to $0.035\pm0.001$ m (both $n=5$; see §6.2b for the metric definition, and §6.2 for the corresponding single-run along-wind figures); $n\ge5$ repeated-trial statistics and a $\tau$-sensitivity sweep confirm the effect is not a single-trial artifact; and the fix passes a 41-scenario regression suite (41/41) with no observed side effects — evidence of harmlessness across the operating envelope rather than of efficacy, which rests on the controlled comparisons above.
4. **A design condition with a stability certificate.** From the MPC's own cost weights alone we derive a damping margin $\zeta$ and, treating the velocity-command saturation as a sector nonlinearity, a circle-criterion condition $\tau K_p<K_v$ under which an explicit quadratic Lyapunov function certifies the interface-consistent loop against that saturation over any bounded command range — giving an admissible plant lag of 0.69 s for an isolated vehicle down to 0.56 s for the stiffest graph tested, against an identified $\tau\approx0.48$ s (§5.6). The reduction the conditions rest on is not assumed but computed from the implemented optimal-control problem: this both verifies that the two controllers command the same acceleration and differ only in realizing it, and corrects the constants, the textbook continuous-time closed form overstating the implemented gains by 17 % and 12 %. We add an input-to-state-stability bound whose gain $\tau/K_p$ is exact rather than estimated, which rules out divergence under any bounded wind and exposes a design trade-off the unforced analysis cannot see: coupling improves disturbance rejection and erodes damping margin at the same time. The sharpest of these results is an invariance: because the controller's gains are solved *at* the identified lag, the damping margin ($\zeta\approx1.11$) and the disturbance gain ($\gamma\approx0.19$ s$^2$) move by under 3 % as $\tau$ ranges over $[0.2,3.0]$ s. **Making the model match the plant does not merely improve the margin; it removes the lag from the margin** — which is the mechanism behind the $\tau$-insensitivity §6.2b measures, and which extends the per-mode analysis to a fleet of vehicles carrying *different* lags, each running the fix tuned to its own. We ground $\zeta$ in the classical delay-margin-versus-Laplacian-eigenvalue theory of networked consensus and establish the nominal stability of the fix. We present these as *design conditions*, not as empirically validated predictions: our controlled topology sweep does not recover the ordering they imply — `star5` and `cross5` come out transposed, and the one pair that holds fleet size fixed resolves in neither direction — while severity instead tracks fleet size (§5.6). Auditing our own coupling measure then shows why: replacing the plain graph Laplacian with the formation's rigidity spectrum leaves three of the four graphs with identical coupling stiffness, so the condition never made a discriminating prediction across them, and the same computation shows three of the four formations are not infinitesimally rigid — which supplies the missing structural reason for the null effect of the formation weight (§6.2c).


**What is and is not new.** The *only* element we do not claim as novel is the lag-identification-and-state-augmentation technique itself, adopted without modification from the single-vehicle literature [Kamel16], [Stastny18]. The contribution lies not in that technique but in three respects that, to our knowledge, prior work does not reach. **(N1) A failure mode, not a tracking penalty.** A velocity-loop lag that the planner's model asserts to be zero does not merely degrade tracking: it produces a *saturation-driven divergence* under wind (median 49 m on a single vehicle), and — the part we have not seen reported — one that low-level retuning does not touch. Tripling the velocity-loop gain measurably improves nominal tracking (38 %, $p=0.036$) while leaving the divergence statistically unchanged ($p=0.39$, $p=0.57$; §6.2d), because a model asserting $\tau=0$ is wrong by 100 % whatever the true lag is. Nor has the effect's *localization* been reported, established here by a non-predictive consensus baseline (§6.5), to the predictive controller's lag-blind internal model rather than to the plant, the coupling, or velocity saturation. **(N2) A structural negative result.** We give what is, to our knowledge, the first explicit argument — with a matched closed-loop confirmation (§6.5) — that an *input-channel* lag is ill-suited to additive disturbance-observer compensation, cleanly separating interface-mismatch error from the exogenous-disturbance error that DOB-MPC and offset-free MPC target. **(N3) A design condition with an absolute-stability certificate.** The closed-form damping margin $\zeta(\tau,\lambda)$, the circle-criterion condition $\tau K_p<K_v$ under which a quadratic Lyapunov function certifies the interface-consistent loop against the velocity-command saturation, the exact-gain ISS bound that accompanies it, the lag-invariance of both (margin and disturbance gain move under 3 % over a fifteen-fold range of $\tau$, which is what carries the analysis to a fleet of heterogeneous lags), and their grounding in the classical delay-margin-versus-Laplacian-eigenvalue theory of consensus (§5.6) give a checkable design rule — including, for a heterogeneous fleet, that it is the *largest* per-vehicle lag that binds. We claim these as design conditions only, report that our own controlled sweep declines to support the ordering they predict, and then show by computing the formation's rigidity spectrum that the ordering was an artifact of the plain-Laplacian coupling proxy rather than a property of the condition: $\lambda_{\max}$ co-varies with fleet size across the graphs tested, the two five-vehicle graphs come out transposed relative to the prediction, and the observed severity tracks fleet size instead (§5.6). We deliberately locate the contribution in the *diagnosis, the assumption-level negative result, the predictive condition, and the system-level demonstration* rather than in the low-level technique, because — as §3.2 establishes in detail — a bare "new augmentation method" claim would not survive comparison with [Kamel16]/[Stastny18], whereas N1–N3 have no such precedent. Separately, and *not* counted among these, we report an implementation-level defect surfaced by the validation regression (the "parked-teammate pull," §6.4) as a practitioner war-story rather than a general finding.


The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. §3 positions this work against distributed formation MPC, single-vehicle lag-augmented MPC, and DOB-MPC literature. §4 summarizes the system under study. §5 presents the diagnosis as a sequence of falsified and confirmed hypotheses. §6 presents the fix and its SITL validation. §7 discusses limitations and practical implications, and §8 concludes.



## 3. Related Work




### 3.1 Distributed / Decentralized Formation MPC



Distributed formation MPC is a mature area; [LiuSurvey24] survey position-, displacement-, distance-, and bearing-based distributed formation methods across UAV, UGV, USV, and AUV platforms, and a complementary recent review [PengReview24] surveys distributed MPC across UAV and vehicle-platoon systems. Among recent representative instances, [XuAccess23] propose a two-layer distributed MPC for leader-follower multi-UAV formation in which each follower reconstructs an assumed predicted trajectory for its neighbours from their last-broadcast state and control sequence — a coupling mechanism structurally close to the one used in this paper (§4) — but validate it only in MATLAB numerical simulation (`fmincon`, no ROS/Gazebo/flight-control firmware, no hardware, one leader plus two followers). The same broadcast-coupling family has been extended to explicit communication anomalies: [LiHu25] maintain formation under delay and packet loss by propagating *assumed prediction states* — structurally the coupling we study, and directly relevant to the communication-dropout scenarios in our regression (§6.3). [XuDai25] give a Lyapunov-based distributed MPC — each vehicle running a three-layer hierarchical controller — for dynamic virtual-leader formation tracking that is fault-tolerant to unexpected actuator faults; we contrast with it in §6.4, where an agent's *silent* failure exposes a different, formation-cost-driven defect that a fault-tolerant design does not target. [Goarin24] propose a decentralized NMPC with exponential control-barrier-function (ECBF) constraints for collision avoidance among independently-goaled quadrotor teams, validated in simulation with up to ten vehicles and on hardware with three; its coupling mechanism (pairwise safety constraints, not a broadcast predicted trajectory or a persistent formation-keeping term) differs from ours, so we cite it as a same-period decentralized-NMPC effort and as evidence that this general research direction has begun reaching real hardware — a point we return to in our limitations (§7.2), since our own controlled validation is SITL-only and our hardware evidence is a single vehicle (§6.6). Crucially, even the most recent representative distributed formation MPC is still built on a nominal vehicle model with no firmware-in-the-loop execution interface: [Drones25] (2025; formation and obstacle soft costs, simulation only) occupies the same cell of Table I as [Erunsal19], so the gap this paper addresses is not a historical artifact. The broadcast-prediction coupling on which this family rests has also been questioned from the field-robotics side: benchmarking against a distributed-MPC trajectory generator, [ZhouEGOSwarm21] argue that such schemes presuppose accurate, high-rate pose communication that real deployments cannot guarantee, and instead share trajectories over a broadcast network built to minimise data transmission and tolerate unreliable links. Our regression addresses that objection only in part — §6.3 exercises the coupling under injected communication delay, dropout and blackout, but in software-in-the-loop; on real radio links the objection stands until flight data is reported (§7.2). The system architecture used in this paper (double-integrator dynamics, tracking/formation/collision-avoidance soft costs, SQP-RTI, broadcast-predicted-trajectory coupling; §4) is a conventional instance of this general framework and is **not claimed as a contribution**; it is described only to the extent needed to support the diagnosis in §5.




### 3.2 Inner-Loop Dynamics Identification and Augmentation into MPC



Identifying a low-level controller's closed-loop or actuator dynamics from flight data and augmenting it into an MPC's internal prediction model is an established single-vehicle technique. [Kamel16] identify a first-order closed-loop attitude response ($\tau_\phi$, $\tau_\theta$) on a real hexacopter and augment it directly into an NMPC state vector. [Stastny18] identify a considerably richer grey-box model for a fixed-wing platform — coupled lateral-directional/longitudinal closed-loop attitude dynamics (ten parameters, fitted and validated with a train/validation/test split and reported RMSE) plus an open-loop velocity-axis model with an explicit first-order throttle-lag time constant — using the MATLAB System Identification Toolbox on real flight data. **This methodological rigor exceeds our own $\tau$-identification procedure** (a single first-order fit from a 130 Hz measurement bypass, §5.4), a limitation we return to in §7.2. [KamelIROS17] extend the same identification-and-augmentation technique to a *multi*-vehicle setting — simulating six MAVs and flying two real AscTec NEO hexacopters — but for reactive, independent-goal collision avoidance rather than formation keeping: there is no virtual leader or geometric shape constraint, and neighbour coupling uses a constant-velocity extrapolation of the neighbour's last known state (Eq. 15 in their paper) rather than a broadcast predicted trajectory. We verified against the full text that [KamelIROS17] does not analyze any interaction between the identified lag and the multi-vehicle avoidance constraints; the lag model appears only in a single-vehicle tracking context, and no oscillation, limit-cycle, or stability analysis is reported for the coupled case. [Erunsal19] instead study true formation keeping — a centralized leader-follower scheme with fixed relative-position references among three vehicles (one physical leader, two followers) — but use only a nominal six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body model with no identified low-level dynamics; an oscillation observed in their simulation is explicitly attributed to sensor noise and model-parameter uncertainty, not to an interface mismatch, and no augmentation or coupling-induced instability is analyzed.

These two papers are complementary rather than overlapping evidence for the gap this paper addresses (Table I). [KamelIROS17] identify and augment the lag but couple reactively; [Erunsal19] couple through a persistent formation cost but do not identify or augment any lag. To our knowledge, no prior work combines the two — identifying and augmenting a low-level lag *and* coupling agents through a persistent formation-keeping cost — which is the architecture in which we observe the failure reported in §5.1. We stress that this is a statement about where the failure was found, not about what causes it: §6.2c shows the divergence survives with every explicit inter-vehicle coupling removed. More broadly, compensating for input delay and actuator lag inside MPC is a well-established topic in its own right (dead-time compensation, Smith-predictor-augmented MPC, and delay-state augmentation), which further underlines that we make no novelty claim for the compensation technique itself; our contribution is the multi-vehicle *diagnosis*, not the single-vehicle *remedy*. A different engineering response to the same lag deserves note: selecting a command interface *below* the laggy loop altogether. This alternative is neither hypothetical nor confined to non-PX4 stacks. The ten-vehicle field swarm of [ZhouSciRob22] runs PX4 itself, on a PX4-standard flight-control unit whose sensor suite is deliberately reduced to the IMU alone, and assigns that unit only the low-level angle control; localization, planning and high-level control all run on the companion computer. The autopilot's velocity loop therefore never enters the tracking path — at the cost of forgoing the firmware's velocity-level failsafe semantics and of reimplementing the outer loops on the companion. Our diagnosis and fix instead address the velocity-setpoint interface that most PX4 offboard deployments, including ours, retain precisely for those safety semantics. That interface is common in deployment-oriented work while its closed-loop behaviour goes unreported: [Pommeranz25] describe a heterogeneous PX4 + ROS 2 swarm architecture on companion hardware of the same class as ours, commanding vehicles in PX4 Offboard mode under GNSS positioning, but report the architecture only: simulation and real-world demonstrations are mentioned without any accompanying tracking, timing or formation measurements.

**The identification-and-learning landscape.** Two threads bracket our own single-fit $\tau$-identification. First, *parametric low-level identification*: [Eschmann24] identify a quadrotor's inertia, thrust curve, and — closest to our concern — a **first-order motor delay** purely from proprioceptive flight data, noting that such low-level delays, though crucial for end-to-end control, are routinely neglected; this is a more recent, same-platform (multirotor, first-order delay) benchmark than [Stastny18], and we return to it in §7.2 as the methodological standard our single 130 Hz fit falls short of. Second, *learned-residual* approaches replace parametric augmentation with a data-driven correction: [Torrente21] fold a Gaussian-process residual into a quadrotor MPC (>100 Hz, up to 70 % tracking-error reduction over the nominal model), and [Kulathunga24] model precisely the residual between a high-level planner and a low-level controller — focusing on the velocity-change (acceleration) channel, the same interface we diagnose — with a sparse GP. We adopt parametric identify-and-augment rather than GP learning for its zero added inference cost and its interpretability (a single physical time constant), but note the learned-residual route as complementary evidence that this planner↔execution interface is worth modelling explicitly (§7.1).

### 3.3 Disturbance-Observer-Based MPC (DOB-MPC)



A different literature line responds to tracking error by estimating and compensating for an exogenous disturbance. [Xu24] propose a fixed-time disturbance observer (FxTDO) that estimates a lumped additive force disturbance $\hat f_d$ in the translational dynamics ($\dot v = \dots + \hat f_d/m$) and feeds it forward into the MPC's prediction model, validated on real hardware under wind and unknown payloads; their observer is explicitly designed for disturbances that are bounded with a bounded derivative (Assumption 1 in their paper) — i.e., slowly varying or quasi-static forcing — and does not target impulsive or high-frequency disturbances. A related extended-state-observer-enhanced LPV-MPC design [NigusieAST26] reports robustness to actuator/motor lags on the order of $\tau=0.03$–$0.08$ s (alongside MEMS sensor noise and Dryden turbulence, single quadrotor, simulation only), roughly an order of magnitude smaller than the $\tau\approx0.48$–$0.65$ s we identify (§5.4) — consistent with the view that small lags fall within reach of ESO-style lumped-disturbance compensation while larger ones (and, more fundamentally, any lag treated as a state-equation disturbance rather than an input-channel remapping) do not; like [Xu24], this design is single-vehicle and reports no formation-coupling interaction. This line remains active: as recently as 2025, [SciChinaGNN25] pair a graph-neural-network wind-effect predictor with a disturbance-aware quadrotor MPC — so the estimate-and-compensate paradigm we contrast against is a current, evolving baseline rather than a straw man.

We do not merely report that our own observer failed empirically (§5.3); we argue *why this class of method is ill-suited* here — taking care not to overstate the argument as an information-theoretic impossibility. FxTDO-style observers model the mismatch as an *additive force term in the state equation* and, critically, assume it is slowly varying relative to the observer/horizon bandwidth (bounded derivative, [Xu24, Assumption 1]; held constant across the prediction horizon). The root cause we identify (§5.4) is instead a mismatch in the *input channel itself*: the MPC's model assumes $\dot v = u$, while the true plant obeys $\dot v = (v_{\mathrm{sp}}-v)/\tau$. The corresponding equivalent disturbance $d_{\mathrm{eq}} = \dot v - u$ is neither exogenous nor quasi-static — it is a state-dependent signal that changes with the commanded and realized velocity at every step — so a compensator that holds $\hat f_d$ constant over the horizon cannot represent it, while its steady-state component is already cancelled by the velocity loop's own integral action (§5.3), leaving essentially nothing for the observer to lock onto at DC. We do **not** claim that no observer whatsoever could help: a sufficiently high-bandwidth estimator of a fast, time-varying $d_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is not ruled out in principle. Nor do we claim the result extends to every adaptive scheme: adaptive-NMPC designs such as [Hanover22] reject an unmodelled payload or aerodynamic force through an L1-style estimate of a *state-equation* disturbance, and our argument concerns exactly that additive-in-the-state-equation class — not the input-channel remapping it does not perform. We scope N2 to that class deliberately. The claim is narrower and, we argue, more useful — the specific quasi-static-disturbance assumption that FxTDO-family DOB-MPC relies on is *violated* by an input-channel lag, so the principled correction is to re-specify the input channel itself: the state-augmentation technique of [Kamel16] and [Stastny18], which we adopt in §6. The same conclusion extends beyond the FxTDO family to the broader plant–model-mismatch MPC literature: offset-free MPC augments the model with integrating *disturbance* states to reject a constant or asymptotically-constant mismatch and eliminate steady-state offset [PannocchiaRawlings03], but this too is an additive state-equation disturbance aimed at an *offset* rather than at an input-channel remapping, and — being predicated on a slowly-varying disturbance — falls under the same argument. Our failure is not a steady-state offset but a saturation-driven limit cycle, so integrating-disturbance/offset-free augmentation does not address it. A direct closed-loop DOB-MPC comparison confirms this in §6.5: an additive observer leaves the wind-forced divergence statistically intact at both a conservative and an aggressive cap, exactly as the command-correlated (not exogenous) nature of the residual predicts.





## 4. System Description



**Plant and low-level control architecture.** The controlled plant is a multirotor (a Holybro X500-class quadrotor, 500 mm wheelbase, in our PX4 v1.14 / Gazebo SITL). Its translational rigid-body dynamics are
$$ m\,\ddot p = f_{\mathrm{th}}\,R(\Theta)\,e_3 - m g\,e_3 + f_{\mathrm{ext}}, $$
where $m$ is the vehicle mass, $f_{\mathrm{th}}\ge0$ the collective thrust, $R(\Theta)\in SO(3)$ the body-to-world rotation, $e_3=[0,0,1]^\top$, $g$ gravity, and $f_{\mathrm{ext}}$ external forces (e.g. wind). PX4 does **not** expose $f_{\mathrm{th}}$ or $\Theta$ to the MPC; it runs a cascade of closed loops — an outer position/velocity controller emitting a thrust-and-attitude setpoint, an attitude loop, an angular-rate loop, and control allocation to the rotors. Commanded in offboard velocity mode (`OffboardControlMode.velocity`), the MPC supplies only a velocity setpoint $v_{\mathrm{cmd}}$, so the entire inner cascade (velocity $\to$ attitude $\to$ rate $\to$ motor) is a *closed-loop subsystem* $\mathcal G:\,v_{\mathrm{cmd}}\mapsto v$ interposed between the MPC and the rigid body. **The plant the MPC actually drives is $\mathcal G$, not the raw rigid body.** The MPC's internal model assumes $\mathcal G$ is the identity (instantaneous velocity realization, $\dot v=u$); §5.4 identifies it instead as a first-order lag, $\dot v=(v_{\mathrm{cmd}}-v)/\tau$ with $\tau\approx0.48$ s, and this paper concerns the consequences of that single modelling gap. We deliberately keep the MPC's own prediction model a double integrator (below), because that assumption is precisely what is under scrutiny.

Each of $N$ ($N\in\{1,\dots,9\}$ in our SITL evaluation) UAVs runs an independent double-integrator MPC over this interface. Per-agent state $x=[p^\top,v^\top]^\top\in\mathbb R^6$ (world-frame NED position and velocity) evolves under the MPC's *assumed* dynamics $\dot p=v,\ \dot v=u$, with $u\in\mathbb R^3$ the commanded acceleration. Each agent solves, at 50 Hz with a receding horizon of $N_h=30$ steps at $\Delta t=0.05$ s ($T=1.5$ s), the nonlinear least-squares OCP
$$
\min_{x_{0:N_h},\,u_{0:N_h-1}} \tfrac12\sum_{k=0}^{N_h-1}\Big[q_p\lVert p_k-p_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert^2 + q_v\lVert v_k-v_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert^2 + r_a\lVert u_k\rVert^2 + w_c\!\!\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}\!\big[\max(0,d_{\mathrm{safe}}-d_{ij,k})\big]^2 + w_f\!\!\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}\!(d_{ij,k}-d_{ij}^\star)^2\Big] + \text{(terminal cost)}
$$
subject to the discretized dynamics and box constraints on $u$ and $v$, solved with acados [Verschueren22] (`SQP_RTI`, `PARTIAL_CONDENSING_HPIPM`). The reference $p_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$ tracks a broadcast virtual leader (with a second-order feedforward term for circular trajectories) plus a static formation offset; the collision term $d_{\mathrm{safe}}$ is a hinge penalty with $w_c=200\gg w_f=0.5$, so collision avoidance strictly dominates formation keeping. **Neighbours are coupled by broadcasting each agent's own predicted position trajectory** $\{p_{j,k}\}_{k=0}^{N_h}$, which is substituted into $d_{ij,k}$ and $d_{ij}^\star$ in place of a single current-position estimate; this is the coupling mechanism referenced in §3.1's comparison with [XuAccess23].

**Interface under study.** The OCP's solution is not applied to the plant directly. PX4 is commanded in velocity-control mode (`OffboardControlMode.velocity=True`): the horizontal components of the MPC's *predicted* state one step ahead, $x_1^{\mathrm{pred}}[3{:}5]$, are sent as a velocity setpoint $v_{\mathrm{cmd}}$ to PX4's internal velocity loop, which the MPC's own dynamics model ($\dot v=u$, i.e. an assumed zero-lag, instantaneous realization) does not represent. This interface — where the OCP's assumed dynamics and the plant's actual low-level dynamics diverge — is the locus of the diagnosis in §5. **Fig. 1:** block diagram, OCP → velocity setpoint → [PX4 velocity loop, first-order lag $\tau$] → realized velocity → state feedback, annotating this interface as the injection point of the mismatch.

**Why PX4 SITL is the appropriate testbed for this particular question.** Because the object of study is a *mismatch between two models of the same interface*, it is worth stating explicitly what a software-in-the-loop environment does and does not stand in for. PX4 SITL and a hardware build are compiled from one and the same flight-control source tree: the velocity loop $\mathcal{G}$ of Fig. 1 — its proportional gain `MPC_XY_VEL_P_ACC`, its output saturation, and the attitude/rate/allocation cascade beneath it — is the identical code, running at the identical rates, in both. We state the version boundary precisely rather than gloss it: the experiments reported here run PX4 v1.14 in SITL, whereas the airframes we target run v1.16. The difference is a hardware constraint rather than a choice — board support for the target flight controller entered PX4 upstream only at v1.16, so no v1.14 build exists for it. The interface under study is present and structurally unchanged across the two — in particular the governing gain `MPC_XY_VEL_P_ACC` sits at its PX4 default of 1.8 on both, which we verified directly on the target vehicles' flight controllers — and every claim we make is about that structure rather than about a version-specific numerical value. What the simulator substitutes is the *plant* around which that firmware closes its loops: rigid-body aerodynamics, motor and ESC dynamics, and sensor noise. The distinction matters here because the two halves of the mismatch fall on opposite sides of it. The half we do claim is that the planner's internal model asserts $\mathcal{G}=\mathrm{I}$ while $\mathcal{G}$ is in fact a lag with a saturation downstream of it — a *structural* property of the firmware and of the OCP, both of which SITL reproduces exactly. The half we do **not** claim from SITL is the numerical value of $\tau$ on any given airframe, since the velocity loop closes around the substituted plant; a different vehicle, payload or firmware tuning will identify a different $\tau$. This is borne out directly: the same loop, at the same gain, identifies $\tau=0.48$ s in SITL and $0.550$–$0.585$ s on the target airframe (§6.6) — the structure transfers, the number does not. This distinction is not a hedge but the paper's own result: §6.2d shows the failure is governed by the *structure* of the mismatch and not by its *magnitude*, because a model asserting $\tau=0$ is wrong by 100 % whatever the true lag happens to be. Two further caveats belong with it: the wind used throughout is a controlled constant lateral force applied in Gazebo rather than turbulence, and no aerodynamic or sensor-degradation effect of real flight is represented. The remaining limitations of SITL-only evidence are stated in §7.2.



## 5. Diagnosis: From Misattribution to Root Cause




### 5.1 The Phenomenon




![fig2_phenomenon](paper_figures/fig2_phenomenon.png)

Circular-trajectory formation scenarios exhibited intermittent tracking degradation flagged as REVIEW by our SITL acceptance gates, and under a physics-level wind disturbance (a constant lateral force applied in Gazebo, calibrated to $\approx$4–8 m/s equivalent wind), the same formations exhibited a growing oscillation reaching a wind-forced peak-to-peak positional error of 33.14 m. **Fig. 2:** phenomenon data — trio3-circle unforced tracking-error time series and cross5-circle wind-forced divergence, drawn from `flight_trio3_circle.csv` and `flight_W1/W2/W3_cross5_circle_*.csv` via the plotting script.


### 5.2 Hypothesis 1 (falsified): An Algorithmic Property



Our first hypothesis was that the oscillation was an artifact of the control law itself. An outer, undamped proportional correction term had been layered on top of the MPC's own predicted velocity output; removing it stabilized the previously oscillatory unforced circular case. However, the oscillation reappeared under wind disturbance even with the outer loop removed, falsifying "algorithmic property" as a complete explanation — the outer loop had only been masking a deeper cause.


### 5.3 Hypothesis 2 (falsified, negative result): External Disturbance




![fig3_dob_negative_result](paper_figures/fig3_dob_negative_result.png)

Our second hypothesis was that an unmodelled exogenous disturbance (wind) was responsible, motivating an online disturbance-observer construction as a candidate remedy. Applied offline to 130 Hz flight data, the observer recovered only 0.017–0.019 m/s² of estimated disturbance against a 1.0 m/s² injected force — a 98 % shortfall — indicating that PX4's velocity loop already rejects constant/slowly-varying forcing at DC through its own integral action, leaving no steady-state exogenous disturbance for an observer to estimate.

This negative result should not be read merely as "our particular observer under-performed." As argued in §3.3, disturbance observers of the FxTDO family estimate an *additive force term in the state equation* ($\dot v = \ldots + \hat f_d/m$) under a quasi-static (slowly-varying) assumption; the true root cause identified next (§5.4) is a mismatch in the *input channel* itself ($\dot v = (v_{\mathrm{sp}}-v)/\tau$ vs. the MPC's assumed $\dot v = u$). The estimate collapses toward zero not because the mismatch is absent, but because its steady-state component is cancelled by the velocity loop's integral action while its dynamic component — a state-dependent, fast-varying equivalent disturbance — violates the quasi-static assumption on which FxTDO-style compensation rests (§3.3). We therefore read this as evidence that DOB-MPC of this family is *ill-suited* to an input-channel lag — not as proof that no observer whatsoever could help — and specify a direct closed-loop comparison (§6.5) to confirm it empirically rather than by argument alone.


### 5.4 Root Cause: Interface Mismatch




![fig4_limit_cycle_partial](paper_figures/fig4_limit_cycle_partial.png)


![fig4b_tau_fit](paper_figures/fig4b_tau_fit.png)

We identified the true root cause by bypassing the MPC and comparing PX4's commanded and realized velocity directly at 130 Hz (5 vehicles, degraded frames excluded; analysis released as `report/tau_goodness.py`). In still air the velocity loop is well fit by a first-order lag $\dot v=(v_{\mathrm{cmd}}-v)/\tau$: the identified model reconstructs the realized velocity almost exactly (forward-integration reconstruction $R^2=0.999$, RMSE $\approx0.02$ m/s over the pre-wind window), with the adopted $\tau=0.48$ s just below the low end of a free-fit spread of $0.49$–$0.65$ s (median 0.55 s, s.d. 0.07 s across fit windows and axes) — all consistent with PX4's own documented tracking-delay range of 0.1–0.5 s (§2). Under wind we deliberately do *not* re-identify $\tau$: the vehicle is then in the very saturation-driven divergence under study, so the commanded-vs-realized relationship is dominated by that divergence rather than the clean velocity loop, and a free first-order fit becomes ill-conditioned (across windows the free-fit $\tau$ swings from negative to several seconds with ODE-residual $R^2\approx0$). The adopted still-air $\tau=0.48$ s nonetheless reconstructs the realized velocity even under wind to $R^2\ge0.96$ (RMSE 0.21 m/s) — adequate for the fix, which §6.2b shows is insensitive to $\tau$ over $[0.4,0.8]$ s. We therefore report a single still-air $\tau$ and treat the earlier impression that $\tau$ "lengthens" under wind as an identification artifact of the divergence, not a change in the physical loop constant (§7.2). The MPC's internal model assumes $\dot v = u$ ($\tau=0$); against the identified plant, this causes the MPC to overestimate its own achievable acceleration by roughly an order of magnitude (nominal OCP bound $a_{\max}=4$ m/s², realized 0.13–0.53 m/s² under the identified lag). Because the MPC repeatedly plans a physically unrealizable recovery action, any perturbation that pushes the commanded-vs-realized velocity gap past the velocity-command saturation threshold ($\approx2$ m of accumulated position error) triggers a self-sustained oscillation whose amplitude and period scale with the velocity limit `max_speed` — a scaling we confirmed directly by comparing two otherwise-identical runs at `max_speed`=3.0 and 5.0 m/s. PX4's own controller documentation [PX4Docs] describes the single-vehicle ingredients directly: position control is a proportional controller acting on position error whose velocity command is then saturated by an explicit velocity limit — so that, absent that limit, a growing position error would command velocities the lagging vehicle cannot realize — which is precisely the bounded single-agent seed of the divergence studied here (§5.5). **A terminological clarification we adopt throughout:** in the *unforced* circular case this behaviour is a genuine saturation-bounded limit cycle (a closed orbit in the error/error-rate phase plane, Fig. 4); under *sustained wind forcing* it is no longer bounded — because the persistently moving reference keeps re-injecting error faster than the saturated command can null it, the position error grows without a fixed bound (individual runs reaching hundreds of metres, §6.2b). We therefore describe the unforced case as a limit cycle and the wind-forced case as a saturation-triggered *divergence*, and avoid using "limit cycle" for the latter; where a single term is needed for both regimes we write "saturation-driven instability." We stress that this is a *parasitic* limit cycle — an unwanted failure mode of the closed loop — not one of the *intentional* limit-cycle references sometimes designed into swarm and formation controllers for circling or reactive obstacle avoidance.



### 5.5 Hypothesis 3 (falsified): Formation Coupling as the Amplifier



The interface mismatch above already destabilizes a *single* vehicle: under wind, a lone agent running the nominal (zero-lag) model does not merely lose tracking accuracy but enters a saturation-driven divergence (solo1, wind-forced peak-to-peak error median **49 m**, §6.2c). The single-vehicle lag is therefore the *seed* of the instability, not a benign effect that coupling alone renders unstable. Our third hypothesis — the one we most expected to hold — was that coupling agents through a *persistent* formation-keeping cost (§4) *amplifies* this already-divergent single-vehicle instability across the swarm: the formation term continuously pulls each agent toward its neighbours' (lag-degraded) predicted or current positions, so a swarm should fare worse than a lone vehicle. The divergence is indeed larger in a five-vehicle formation ($\approx$2.1$\times$ per-agent). But the controlled decomposition below (§6.2c) does **not** support the mechanism: removing the formation-keeping weight changes nothing measurable, removing the collision term as well changes nothing measurable, and a five-independent-solo null model — no shared leader, frame, or coupling of any kind — reproduces the amplification in full. We therefore report this hypothesis as falsified — the third of three — and make no claim that formation coupling is the amplifier.


![fig5_propagation](paper_figures/fig5_propagation.png)

As argued in §3.2, this is not simply a case the prior literature happened not to test. [KamelIROS17] combine the same lag-identification-and-augmentation technique with a *reactive* coupling scheme — a pairwise avoidance constraint, active only near a predicted conflict and based on a constant-velocity extrapolation of the neighbour's state — which imposes no persistent, always-active bias toward a *shared* reference. We are careful not to over-claim here: our own decomposition (§6.2c) falsifies the mechanism we had expected — the multi-vehicle increase survives with the formation weight switched off, and then with the collision term switched off as well — so we do not claim the reactive/persistent distinction, or any other coupling ingredient, as the operative one. The contrast with [KamelIROS17] is therefore about *architecture*, not mechanism. [Erunsal19] combine true formation keeping — a persistent bias toward a fixed relative-position reference, structurally identical in kind to ours — with an unidentified nominal dynamics model, so the interface mismatch that would drive the interaction is simply absent from their model. The architecture in which we observe the failure combines *both* ingredients (Table I): a persistent, always-active coupling bias, and a state-augmented model whose assumed lag can desynchronize from the plant's true lag. To our knowledge this combination has not previously been instantiated. We record that as the architectural setting in which we encountered the failure, and explicitly *not* as an account of why it has gone unreported — §6.2c shows the divergence arises on a *single* vehicle carrying no formation cost at all, so neither ingredient of Table I is necessary for it, and why it has not been reported before remains an open question. We further isolate the second ingredient: a memoryless consensus law carrying the *same* persistent formation coupling but *no* internal prediction model does not exhibit the limit cycle even when its gain is raised until it saturates more deeply than the diverging MPC (§6.5). The instability therefore requires not merely the lagged plant, but a *predictive* controller whose internal model is blind to that lag — sharpening the second ingredient of Table I from "a lag is identified and augmented" to "a receding-horizon model is *wrong* about the lag." **Fig. 5:** multi-vehicle track-error time series (5 drones, wind-forced, legacy model, from `flight_exp4_A_legacy.csv`) showing the correlated cross-vehicle divergence described above.


**A controlled decomposition of the multi-vehicle increase (completed; see §6.2c for the data):**

To separate the single-vehicle *seed* from any multi-vehicle *amplification*, we ran — under the identical legacy ($\tau_{\mathrm{model}}=0$), wind-forced protocol, $n=5$, medians reported given the strong right-skew of §6.2b — a single vehicle and a formation-weight sweep (data and figures in §6.2c). The two statistics used (worst-agent; per-agent = per-run mean over vehicles of each vehicle's wind-window peak-to-peak, medians over $n=5$ runs) are defined in §6.2c; all values pass a per-vehicle validity screen that discards non-measurements only (frozen health telemetry, state-estimator overflow, or a vehicle parked in the hover fallback, whose reported error is then stale — never an extreme but genuinely flown outcome), and every number is recomputed from the archived logs by the released scripts (`report/wf_significance.py`, `report/m1_provenance.py`). **(i) Single-vehicle seed:** solo1 diverges to a wind-window peak-to-peak of median 49 m (range 45–55), and the interface-consistent fix collapses it to 0.24 m — establishing that the mechanism, and its remedy, already operate on one vehicle. **(ii) Implicit coupling:** five vehicles with the formation weight set to zero ($w_f=0$) — still sharing a common leader reference, world frame, and collision term — diverge to a worst-agent median of 125 m, per-agent median 102 m — roughly 2.1$\times$ the single-vehicle seed — so the amplification survives *with the explicit formation cost switched off*. **(iii) Explicit formation weight:** restoring and increasing $w_f$ (0.25 / 0.5 / 1.0 / 2.0) moves the *worst-agent* median to 112 / 153 / 169 / 144 m but the *typical* agent barely at all (per-agent medians 88.7 / 101.4 / 104.4 / 104.4 m — non-monotonic, with $w_f{=}0.25$ sitting *below* $w_f{=}0$'s 101.9 m); a two-sided Mann–Whitney test of $w_f$ on ($w_f=0.5$) vs. off ($w_f=0$) on the per-agent statistic ($n=5$ vs. 5) gives $p=0.69$, Cliff's $\delta=+0.20$. **(iv) No explicit coupling at all:** removing the formation weight *and* the collision term together ($w_f=w_c=0$) leaves the divergence statistically unchanged — worst-agent median 131 m (vs. 125 m at $w_f=0$), per-agent median 96.9 m (vs. 102 m; $p=0.84$, Cliff's $\delta=+0.12$) — and still significantly above the single vehicle ($p=0.008$, per-agent), which falsifies the hypothesis outright. **(v) Nothing shared at all:** a five-independent-solo null model — run on a second host with matched same-host solo and coupled arms, and reported in full in §6.2c (Fig. 9) — reproduces the coupled five-vehicle divergence to within 1 % with *zero* inter-vehicle information flow. We therefore state the claim in its resolved form: *the single-vehicle velocity-loop lag already diverges under wind; a five-vehicle simulation amplifies it ($\approx$2.1$\times$ per-agent on the primary host, $\approx$1.6$\times$ on the secondary), but the null model reproduces that amplification in full — it is a property of the multi-vehicle scenario, not of any inter-vehicle coupling (§6.2c); and the interface-consistent fix eliminates the divergence at every level, from one vehicle (49$\to$0.24 m) to the full formation (§6.2).* This corrects our initial framing (that a benign single-vehicle lag was rendered unstable only by the formation cost) and, by anchoring the fix at the single-vehicle level, strengthens rather than weakens its generality.


### 5.6 A Design Condition and a Stability Certificate



The preceding sections establish empirically *that* the velocity-loop lag destabilizes the loop, and that the instability is not removed by low-level retuning. We now ask *when* — for what combination of identified lag, formation weight, and coupling topology — the interface-consistent controller of §6.1 retains a stability margin, using only the MPC's own cost weights (§4) and standard linear systems tools, without new experiments. The answer turns out to be more interesting than the question: the fixed loop's margin is *insensitive* to the identified lag, and that insensitivity is the precise sense in which the fix works. We offer what follows as a *design condition* and nothing more: §6.2c's falsification of the coupling hypothesis, and the controlled sweep reported at the end of this section — which does not recover the ordering an earlier form of the condition implied — together mean it is not a validated explanation of the observed severities.

**Linearized closed-loop model.** What the interface-consistent controller emits is a *velocity setpoint* — its decision variable is the setpoint itself, and that is what is published (§4) — so the per-mode loop must be written in those terms. Away from the (hinge-activated, near-collision-only) collision term, the controller's small-signal feedback is $u=-K_p e-K_v\dot e$ with $K_p$ in s$^{-1}$ and $K_v$ dimensionless, and the plant is the identified first-order lag $\dot v=(u-v)/\tau$. Since $\dot e=v$ in error coordinates, eliminating $v$ gives, per mode, a damped second-order error dynamic
$$\ddot e + \frac{1+K_v}{\tau}\,\dot e + \frac{K_p}{\tau}\,e = 0,\qquad \zeta(\tau,\nu)=\frac{1+K_v}{2\sqrt{\tau K_p}},$$
where the "$1$" added to $K_v$ is the plant's own velocity feedback and is dimensionless, as it must be. Linearizing the persistent formation cost $w_f\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}(d_{ij}-d_{ij}^\star)^2$ about the nominal shape and projecting onto a scalar mode adds a *stiffness to the stage cost*, not to the gain: the modal position weight becomes $q_p+w_f\nu$, where $\nu\ge0$ is an eigenvalue of the formation's coupling operator ($\nu=0$ is the common leader-following mode, unaffected by formation coupling). We take $\nu=\mu\in\operatorname{spec}(R^\top R)$, the formation's rigidity spectrum; assumption A2 below records that an earlier form of this analysis used the plain graph Laplacian's $\lambda$ instead, and the audit after the sweep shows what that substitution costs. $K_p$ and $K_v$ then follow from the modal weights by solving the controller's own optimal-control problem, which is the subject of the next paragraph.

**Scope: three simplifying assumptions (A1--A3).** We state the boundary of the analysis before the results rather than after. **(A1)** The linearization drops the collision-avoidance hinge term, valid away from near-collision configurations. **(A2)** Projecting the formation cost onto a single scalar mode per eigenvalue ignores the direction of each pairwise baseline. An earlier form of this analysis measured coupling by the plain graph Laplacian's $\lambda$; the falsifiable ordering stated below, and the sweep that tests it, both belong to that form and we leave them as they were written. The audit after the sweep computes the formation's rigidity spectrum instead and finds the plain Laplacian overstates the denser graphs by up to a factor of two — enough to dissolve the ordering, and (as the certificate numbers below record) enough to decide whether the densest graph passes the Proposition 2 certificate at all. We therefore quote $\mu$ as primary and $\lambda$ where the historical prediction is at issue, and state the prediction, test it, and re-examine it in that order rather than quietly substituting the better measure. **(A3)** The analysis holds $\tau$ fixed at the plant's *true* identified value — i.e., it characterizes the margin of the already-fixed, interface-consistent controller (§6.1) as a function of topology, not the mechanism behind the much larger, legacy zero-lag-assumption divergence of §5.1/§6.2, which is a distinct internal-model-mismatch effect not modelled here. We report what follows as a design condition on the fixed controller, not as a complete stability proof, and not as an explanation of the legacy divergence's magnitude.

*(The gain bookkeeping behind these numbers, the legacy loop's contrasting closed form, the role assumption A2 plays in the certificate, and the connections to classical delay-margin and robust-control readings are collected in Appendix B; nothing in the main line depends on them.)*

**The margin is lag-invariant, and that is what the fix buys.** Because $K_p$ and $K_v$ are themselves solved *at* the identified $\tau$, they scale with it, and the $\tau$ in $\zeta=(1+K_v)/2\sqrt{\tau K_p}$ cancels almost exactly. Sweeping $\tau$ over a fifteen-fold range, $\zeta$ moves only from $1.133$ at $\tau=0.2$ s to $1.103$ at $\tau=3.0$ s — a $2.7\,\%$ spread — while the equivalent acceleration gains stay within $0.9\,\%$ ($k_p^{\mathrm{eff}}$) and $3.2\,\%$ ($k_v^{\mathrm{eff}}$) and the disturbance gain of Proposition 3 stays within $1\,\%$. **The interface-consistent loop is, to within a few percent, the same closed loop whatever the plant's lag** — which is exactly what "the controller's model matches the plant" ought to mean, and which we had not previously stated. It also supplies the mechanism for an empirical result §6.2b reports without one: that the fix's tracking error is flat in the *assumed* $\tau$ across $[0.4,0.8]$ s. What does tighten with $\tau$ is not the damping margin but the *certificate* of Proposition 2, which fails for an isolated vehicle at $\tau\approx0.69$ s.

One consequence is worth recording here because §7.2 asserts it without proof. A heterogeneous fleet — vehicles with different lags $\tau_i$, each running an interface-consistent controller tuned to its own — is homogenized by the fix: the realized closed loops agree to within $1\,\%$ and $3\,\%$ across $\tau_i\in[0.2,3.0]$ s. The per-mode decomposition, which would otherwise require identical vehicles, therefore remains valid for such a fleet, and fleet-wide stability reduces to each vehicle satisfying its own certificate — so it is indeed **the largest $\tau_i$ that binds**, a claim we can now support rather than assert.

Evaluated per topology at $\tau=0.48$ s and $w_f=0.5$, with coupling measured by the rigidity spectrum, the modal weights $q_p+w_f\mu$ give $K_p=2.53/3.02/2.95/3.02/3.02$ s$^{-1}$ and $K_v=1.46/1.55/1.54/1.55/1.55$ for an isolated vehicle and `trio3`/`star5`/`cross5`/`grid9`, hence $\zeta=1.11/1.06/1.07/1.06/1.06$. Every value is above one — which is what Proposition 3's exact-gain argument requires — and, as the audit after the sweep explains, they are nearly equal because three of the four graphs turn out to carry identical coupling stiffness. Under the superseded plain-Laplacian proxy the same quantities read $\zeta=1.11/1.06/1.05/1.03/1.02$: still above one, but with `grid9` close enough to the edge that, as recorded below, it *fails* the Proposition 2 certificate under that measure and passes under the rigidity spectrum. That is the sharpest practical consequence of A2 in this paper.

**What this does and does not explain.** For every $\tau,\nu\ge0$ this system is linearly stable (both coefficients stay positive), so this small-signal model — like any linear analysis of this loop — cannot by itself reproduce the large-amplitude limit cycle of §5.1/§6.2; that requires the velocity-saturation nonlinearity of §5.4, which sets the oscillation's amplitude scale — while its *driver*, the predictive controller's over-commanding against a lag-blind internal model, we make precise in the robustness-view aside of Appendix B. What $\zeta$ does provide is a **stability margin computed from the controller's own cost weights**: mildly overdamped, essentially independent of the plant lag, and — under the correct coupling measure — nearly independent of topology as well. The margin still falls with coupling stiffness, but across the four graphs actually tested that fall is under $1\,\%$; the quantity that does move with $\tau$, and therefore the one a designer must check, is the absolute-stability certificate of Proposition 2.

**Nominal stability of the interface-consistent fix (Proposition).** The interface-consistent controller (§6.1) adopts the *correct* velocity model $\dot v=(v_{\mathrm{sp}}-v)/\tau$, so the per-mode error dynamics above *are* its closed loop. Its characteristic polynomial is $s^2+\tfrac{1+K_v}{\tau}\,s+\tfrac{K_p}{\tau}$. Because $\tau>0$, $1+K_v>0$, and $K_p>0$ for every $\tau>0$ and every modal weight $q_p+w_f\nu>0$, both coefficients are strictly positive; for a second-order polynomial this is the necessary and sufficient Routh–Hurwitz condition for asymptotic stability. **The fix is therefore nominally asymptotically stable at every formation topology and every identified lag**, with the guaranteed margin quantified by $\zeta$ — and, by the lag-invariance above, that margin is essentially the same margin at every lag. This is a statement about the *fixed* loop; the legacy loop, whose internal model is wrong, is analysed in the robustness-view aside of Appendix B.

**Absolute stability under velocity-command saturation.** The proposition above is a *linear* statement, and the failure mode of §5.4 is driven by the velocity-command saturation — so a stability claim that ignores that nonlinearity leaves the decisive element unaddressed. We therefore strengthen it. Treat the saturation as a memoryless nonlinearity in the sector $[0,1]$ (which $\mathrm{sat}(\cdot)$ satisfies, since $\mathrm{sat}(u)/u\in(0,1]$), and place it at the controller output. Per mode the loop transfer of the interface-consistent controller — from the saturated velocity command to the position error — is
$$L(s)=\frac{K_1+K_2 s}{s(\tau s+1)},\qquad K_1=K_p,\quad K_2=K_v,$$
whose real part evaluates in closed form to
$$\operatorname{Re}L(j\omega)=\frac{K_2-K_1\tau}{1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}}.$$
The circle criterion for a sector-$[0,1]$ nonlinearity requires $\operatorname{Re}L(j\omega)>-1$ for all $\omega$. The expression above is monotone in $\omega^{2}$, taking its extreme value at $\omega=0$ and tending to $0$ as $\omega\to\infty$, so its infimum over $\omega$ is $\min\{K_2-K_1\tau,\,0\}$ and the requirement collapses to $K_2-K_1\tau>-1$ — a single closed-form condition on the design constants:
$$\boxed{\ \tau\,K_p\;<\;K_v+1\ }$$
**Read as a *global* statement over the whole sector $[0,1]$, however, this is more than the loop can support, and we correct it rather than restate it.** Because $K_p$ and $K_v$ are themselves functions of $\tau$, the condition is implicit and its admissible-lag boundary must be solved for rather than read off; taken at face value the boxed inequality would give $\tau<0.97$ s for an isolated vehicle and $0.84/0.86/0.84/0.84$ s for `trio3`/`star5`/`cross5`/`grid9`. But the zero-lower-bound branch of the circle criterion requires the open loop to be Hurwitz, and $L(s)$ is not — it carries the integrator of the velocity-to-position channel. That hypothesis cannot be recovered by an indented-Nyquist argument, because absolute stability over the *full* sector $[0,1]$ is unattainable here for a reason independent of any criterion: the zero map $\varphi\equiv0$ belongs to that sector, and it leaves the integrator unfed, so the origin of the resulting loop is not asymptotically stable. The boxed inequality is therefore the limiting form of the certificate, not the certificate; the indentation argument is valid for every strictly positive lower sector bound and fails only in the limit, where the critical set stops being a bounded disk. The next paragraph derives the certificate itself, in Lyapunov form.

**Lyapunov form of the certificate (Proposition 2).** The circle criterion is a frequency-domain surrogate for a quadratic Lyapunov function; making that surrogate explicit both repairs the hypothesis and sharpens the condition. Restrict the sector to $[\kappa,1]$ with $\kappa\in(0,1]$ — the velocity-command saturation satisfies it on the set $\{|u|\le v_{\max}/\kappa\}$, so $\kappa$ parameterizes how deeply the command is allowed to be driven into saturation. Two facts then hold for every $\kappa>0$. First, the loop closed with the *linear* gain $\kappa$ has characteristic polynomial $\tau s^{2}+(1+\kappa K_v)s+\kappa K_p$, whose coefficients are strictly positive, so it is Hurwitz and the criterion's remaining hypothesis is met. Second, the critical disk $D(\kappa,1)$ — diameter $-1/\kappa$ to $-1$ on the real axis — lies entirely in $\{\operatorname{Re}\le-1\}$, whereas the closed-form real part above is strictly positive at every frequency exactly when
$$\boxed{\ \tau\,K_p\;<\;K_v\ }$$
under which the whole Nyquist locus sits in the open right half-plane, hence outside $D(\kappa,1)$ for *every* $\kappa$ simultaneously, with no encirclements (the locus runs from $-j\infty$ as $\omega\to0$ to $0$ as $\omega\to\infty$, and the indented contour's large arc stays in the right half-plane as noted above). By the Kalman--Yakubovich--Popov lemma applied to the multiplier $(1+L)/(1+\kappa L)$ — Hurwitz by the first fact and of real part $\ge1$ by the second, hence strictly positive real — there exists $P_\kappa\succ0$ such that $V(x)=x^{\top}P_\kappa x$ decreases strictly along the loop for *every* memoryless nonlinearity in the sector $[\kappa,1]$, with $\{V\le c\}\subseteq\{|u|\le v_{\max}/\kappa\}$ an ellipsoidal estimate of the region of attraction. **The certificate is therefore regional rather than global**: $\kappa$ may be taken arbitrarily small, so the certified command range is arbitrarily large, but $P_\kappa$ degenerates as $\kappa\to0$ and no single quadratic form certifies the whole state space — which is precisely what the $\varphi\equiv0$ obstruction above demands.

The certified condition is strictly tighter than the boxed limiting form (it drops the $+1$), and it is the same quantity the describing-function consistency check below evaluates: at the identified $\tau=0.48$ s, $K_v-\tau K_p$ equals $0.240$ for an isolated vehicle and $0.099/0.121/0.099/0.099$ for `trio3`/`star5`/`cross5`/`grid9` — positive throughout. Solving the implicit boundary $\tau K_p(\tau)=K_v(\tau)$ gives the admissible lags themselves: **$0.69$ s for an isolated vehicle and $0.56/0.58/0.56/0.56$ s for the four formations**, so the identified $\tau\approx0.48$ s sits inside the certificate at every topology, by a factor $1.44$ isolated and $1.16$ at the binding graphs.

**Disturbance rejection: an ISS bound with an exact gain (Proposition 3).** Propositions 1--2 are statements about the *unforced* loop, whereas every experiment in this paper is wind-forced — so we close that gap inside the same per-mode model. Let the disturbance enter as an equivalent acceleration $d(t)$ on the error dynamics, $\ddot e+\tfrac{1+K_v}{\tau}\dot e+\tfrac{K_p}{\tau}e=d$. Because the state matrix is Hurwitz (Proposition 1), the system is input-to-state stable and $V(x)=x^{\top}Px$ with $A^{\top}P+PA=-I$ is an ISS-Lyapunov function; what is worth stating is not that fact but the *gain*, which here is available exactly rather than as a bound. The damping ratio exceeds one at every topology and identified lag considered here ($\zeta=1.06$–$1.11$; equivalently $(1+K_v)^{2}>4\tau K_p$), so both poles are real and negative and the impulse response of the $d\mapsto e$ channel is non-negative for all $t\ge0$. Its $L^{1}$ norm therefore coincides with its DC gain, and the induced $L^{\infty}\to L^{\infty}$ gain is exactly that:
$$\|e\|_{\infty}\;\le\;\beta(\|x_0\|,t)\;+\;\gamma\,\|d\|_{\infty},\qquad \gamma=\frac{\tau}{K_p},$$
giving, at $\tau=0.48$ s, $\gamma=0.189$ s$^{2}$ for an isolated vehicle and $0.159/0.163/0.159/0.159$ s$^{2}$ for `trio3`/`star5`/`cross5`/`grid9`. Like $\zeta$, this gain is lag-invariant: it stays within $1\,\%$ of $0.19$ s$^{2}$ across $\tau\in[0.2,3.0]$ s. **The fix therefore fixes not only the damping but the disturbance sensitivity, at a value the plant's lag does not move** — which is the quantitative content of "the model matches the plant". Two consequences follow that the unforced propositions cannot state. **(a) The bound does not grow with how long the disturbance persists**: no bounded wind, however sustained, can drive the interface-consistent loop to divergence. That is precisely the qualitative dichotomy the experiments exhibit — a legacy loop running away to a median peak-to-peak of 153 m against a fixed loop that settles at 0.61 m (§6.2) — and it is a property the legacy loop, whose internal model is wrong, has no claim to. **(b) Coupling buys disturbance rejection with stability margin**: $\gamma$ decreases in $\nu$ while $\zeta$ decreases in $\nu$ as well, so a stiffer formation graph rejects a given disturbance better while sitting closer to the critically-damped edge and closer to the Proposition 2 bound — the `grid9` failure under the $\lambda$ proxy above is precisely this trade run past its limit. A design that adds coupling to improve rejection therefore spends margin to buy it, and the two boxed conditions say how much.

One honesty note accompanies this result as well: we do *not* use $\gamma$ to predict a measured error. The wind in these experiments is a constant lateral force applied in Gazebo (§4), and the reduced first-order interface $\dot v=(v_{\mathrm{sp}}-v)/\tau$ abstracts an inner loop that carries its own integral action against exactly such a constant force; where the disturbance effectively enters this reduced model, and with what magnitude, is not identified here. $\gamma$ is therefore a *design* quantity for comparing $(\tau,\nu,w_f)$ choices within the model, not a predictor of the metres reported in §6.2 — a distinction we draw explicitly, having already reported one condition of our own (the $\zeta$ ordering) that a controlled experiment declined to support.

**A numerical check of all three propositions.** The propositions above are analytic; we close §5.6 by simulating the same per-mode loop and reporting what the analysis gets right and where it is loose (`report/make_fig5b.py`; a saturated two-state ODE, no SITL data). Fig. 5b reports three things.

**(a) Proposition 2 is sound but never binding here.** Over a grid of 48 $(\tau,\nu)$ points spanning $\tau\in[0.3,8]$ s and $\nu\in[0,300]$ — far beyond anything this system runs — the saturated loop converges from a 20 m initial error at *every* point, while the certificate holds at only four of them. We had previously reported this conservatism as "convergence to $\tau\ge2.4$ s"; the accurate statement is stronger and less flattering to the certificate: **we could not make this loop unstable at all** — not by lengthening the lag to 8 s, not by stiffening the coupling by two orders of magnitude, and not by mis-tuning the controller's assumed lag against the plant's (a $5\times$ mismatch either way still converges). The reason is structural: the saturation limits the *velocity* command, and velocity is exactly what reduces position error, so the loop cannot wind up the way an acceleration-saturated one can. This is the same conclusion this section reaches from the other side, and it bears repeating: the divergence of §5.1/§6.2 is not a phenomenon of the static-equivalent loop, and no tightening of this analysis will reproduce it.

**(b) Proposition 3's gain is exact, not merely a bound.** Under a constant disturbance the simulated $\sup|e|$ equals $\gamma\|d\|_\infty$ to four decimal places across $d\in[0.5,5]$ m/s$^2$. Under a square-wave disturbance the bound is respected and approached from below — ratios $1.000$ at a 40 s period, $0.999$ at 10 s and $0.639$ at 2 s — which is what the non-negative impulse response predicts: the $L^\infty$ induced gain is attained by constant inputs and by nothing else. This is the one place in §5.6 where a prediction is confirmed to the last reported digit rather than merely not contradicted.

**(c) The lag-invariance is not an artifact of the identified value.** $\zeta$ and $\gamma$ are plotted against $\tau$ over a fifteen-fold range with the controller re-solved at each $\tau$; both are flat to within $3\,\%$ and $1\,\%$, and the Proposition 2 boundary at $0.69$ s passes through the middle of that flat region without anything happening to either curve — a compact statement of the section's main structural result and of the certificate's conservatism at once.

**A concrete, falsifiable topology ordering.** Using the exact neighbour graphs already defined for this system's `trio3`, `star5`, `cross5`, and `grid9` scenarios (§4, `config/scenarios.yaml`), the formation-Laplacian's largest eigenvalue is $\lambda_{\max}=3$ (trio3, complete graph $K_3$), $3.618$ (star5, 5-cycle ring), $5$ (cross5, hub-and-spoke star $K_{1,4}$), and $6$ (grid9, $3\times3$ grid graph $P_3\square P_3$) — a strict ordering trio3 $<$ star5 $<$ cross5 $<$ grid9. Substituting into $\zeta$ at $\tau=0.48$ s gives $\zeta\approx1.11$ (isolated agent, $\nu=0$), $1.06$ (trio3), $1.05$ (star5), $1.03$ (cross5), $1.02$ (grid9): a monotonically shrinking margin in the same order. **This predicts that, under an equal disturbance, grid9 should exhibit the most severe/persistent oscillation, followed by cross5, star5, and trio3 least** — a concrete, quantitative and falsifiable *ordering*, which we test against a controlled matched-protocol sweep below, and which, we report in advance, that sweep does not bear out. The prediction is inherited from the plain-Laplacian proxy of A2; we state it in that form because that is the form in which it was made and tested, and examine the proxy itself after the sweep. Two caveats must be read with that comparison, and we state them before the data rather than after: (i) $\zeta$ characterizes the *interface-consistent* loop (simplifying assumption A3), whereas the sweep is necessarily run on the *legacy* loop, where the divergence is large enough to measure — so the sweep tests the predicted ordering, not the predicted margin of the loop it was derived for; and (ii) across these four graphs $\lambda_{\max}$ co-varies with fleet size (3, 5, 5 and 9 vehicles), so only the `star5`-vs-`cross5` pair holds vehicle count fixed. The spread being tested is also small — $\zeta$ ranges over $1.02$–$1.06$ across the four formations — which is a further reason, independent of the audit below, to expect the sweep to be an insensitive test.

**Controlled empirical test ($n=5$ per topology, matched protocol) — the predicted ordering is not recovered.** An initial exploratory pass ($n=2$ per topology, each formation left at its own pre-existing leader-circle speed) supported the prediction only coarsely — the sparsest topology (`trio3`) was clearly least affected, but the finer ordering among the denser topologies did not resolve, plausibly because leader speed was not matched across formations. To remove that confound we ran the same legacy ($\tau_{\mathrm{model}}=0$), wind-forced protocol with leader circling speed and recording duration matched across all four topologies (0.5 m/s, 300 s), $n=5$ independent repetitions per topology, and one and the same controller and firmware build throughout. **Why the ordering was probably not there to find: checking assumption A2.** Having reported a negative result against the proxy's prediction, we check the proxy itself rather than leave it standing as a caveat (`report/verify_rigidity_spectrum.py`; a $2n\times2n$ eigenvalue computation from the nominal shapes in `config/scenarios.yaml`, no simulation). Linearizing $w_f\sum(d_{ij}-d_{ij}^\star)^2$ about the nominal shape gives $w_f\,\delta p^\top R^\top R\,\delta p$, where $R$ is the rigidity matrix — planar, because the implementation measures inter-vehicle distances in $xy$ only — so the consistent substitute for $\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(L)$ is $\mu\in\operatorname{spec}(R^\top R)$. It changes the picture entirely: $\mu_{\max}=3.00$ (trio3), $2.50$ (`star5`), $3.00$ (`cross5`), $3.00$ (`grid9`), against $\lambda_{\max}=3$, $3.618$, $5$, $6$. **Three of the four graphs have identical maximum coupling stiffness, and the fourth is the lowest of all.** The proxy overstates the denser graphs by up to a factor of two, because it credits every edge with a full unit of stiffness in every direction whereas an edge constrains only its own baseline direction. Substituted into the damping margin, $\zeta$ reads $1.058/1.066/1.058/1.058$ across trio3/`star5`/`cross5`/`grid9` — a spread of $0.8\,\%$, against the $3.4\,\%$ the Laplacian proxy gives, and with `star5` now the *largest* rather than the second-smallest. **The design condition, evaluated correctly, makes essentially no discriminating prediction across these four formations**, so the sweep's failure to recover the strict Laplacian ordering is evidence against A2 rather than against $\zeta$. The correction is not merely presentational: as Proposition 2's numbers record, `grid9`'s admissible lag is $0.46$ s under $\lambda$ — *below* the identified $0.48$ s — and $0.56$ s under $\mu$, so the choice of coupling measure decides whether the densest configuration tested is certified at all. We are careful not to convert this into a rescue: the rigidity spectrum does not predict the observed severities either — it would place `star5` least affected, and `star5` is the more severe member of the fleet-size-matched pair — so $\zeta$ remains unvalidated as a predictor of severity, and what the data track is still fleet size. What the check does establish is that the *experiment was never capable of testing the condition on these four graphs*, and that a future test would have to be designed around $\mu$, on formations chosen to separate it.

The same computation returns a second result we did not anticipate. **Three of the four formations are not infinitesimally rigid in the plane**: $\operatorname{rank}R$ is $5$, $4$ and $12$ against the $2n-3=7$, $7$ and $15$ that `star5`, `cross5` and `grid9` would need (only trio3 — a triangle carrying all three of its edges — is minimally rigid). The formation cost therefore leaves $2$, $3$ and $3$ zero-cost deformation modes respectively: **it does not determine the formation's shape.** The shape is held primarily by each vehicle's own tracking of its leader-referenced offset, with the inter-vehicle term adding stiffness only in those directions the graph happens to constrain — and largely duplicating, in those directions, a tracking cost that already pins each vehicle's position. This supplies a structural reason for a null result we had previously reported without one: the eight-fold sweep of $w_f$ in §6.2c moves the typical agent's divergence hardly at all ($p=0.69$), and removing the formation term outright changes nothing measurable. On an under-constrained graph, that is what a formation-keeping cost should be expected to do. We note that formation objectives which constrain the *shape itself* rather than a set of pairwise distances already exist: [QuanTRO23] plan swarm formations against a differentiable graph-based similarity metric — invariant to translation, rotation and scaling, and validated on outdoor swarm hardware — and replacing the distance-based term with a metric of that family is the natural remedy this rigidity computation points to. We leave it as future work, since every experiment in this paper holds the formation cost fixed.

**A collision-weight control.** The four scenarios were not initially as matched as the comparison above requires: the `cross5` scenario had carried a collision weight $w_c=500$ from the day it was defined, against the default $w_c=200$ used by the other three, and the collision term is demonstrably active in these runs (window-minimum inter-vehicle spacing falls below $d_{\mathrm{safe}}=1.5$ m in most of them). We therefore re-ran `cross5` at $w_c=200$ with nothing else changed, $n=5$; that is the `cross5` row tabulated above. The weight makes no detectable difference to the outcome (per-agent median 94.2 m at $w_c=200$ against 104.0 m at $w_c=500$; two-sided $p=0.69$, Cliff's $\delta=+0.20$), so the transposition is not an artifact of it. It does affect the *spread* — the $w_c=500$ runs are markedly tighter (per-agent range $[101.1,107.6]$ m against $[91.8,182.2]$ m) — which is a useful reminder of how little $n=5$ resolves at this variance.

## 6. Interface-Consistent MPC: Fix and Validation



**6.1 Method.** Following [Kamel16] and [Stastny18], we fold the identified first-order lag into the OCP's own prediction model, replacing the assumed dynamics $\dot v=u$ with $\dot v=(v_{\mathrm{sp}}-v)/\tau$ and outputting the raw control input $u_0=v_{\mathrm{sp}}$ directly (rather than a one-step-ahead predicted state) as the velocity command, with $\tau\approx0.5$ s from the identification in §5.4. **We do not claim novelty in this technique**; the contribution is the diagnosis that motivates it, and the demonstration that a single, physically identified constant is sufficient — no observer, no retuning, no added inference cost — to remove the failure at every fleet size we tested, from one vehicle to nine (§6.2, §6.2c).

![fig8_fix_money](paper_figures/fig8_fix_money.png)

**6.2 SITL validation.** Under the same wind-forcing protocol as §5.1, the fix reduces the wind-forced along-wind peak-to-peak positional error from 33.14 m to 0.13 m and the along-wind standard deviation from 8.28 m to 0.02 m (both are per-vehicle statistics averaged over the five vehicles for $t\ge110$ s, and are reproducible from the released CSVs via `report/headline_provenance.py`), with zero safety violations and zero controller fallbacks (vs. 15 and 106 respectively before the fix). Under the unforced circular-trajectory case (no wind, `safety_filter` active in both conditions), steady-state tracking error falls from 0.368 m to 0.035 m (mean) and from 0.815 m to 0.114 m (max) — moving the scenario from REVIEW to PASS under our 0.5 m acceptance threshold — without retuning any cost weight. A secondary, physically-grounded discrepancy independently flagged during visual inspection (one vehicle appearing higher than another in a three-vehicle circular formation) was traced to the same interface-consistent model correcting an EKF-invisible altitude estimation bias, reducing the Gazebo-ground-truth inter-vehicle height difference from a mean of 1.04 m (peak 1.30 m) to 0.12 m (peak 0.35 m) — an $\approx$8.7$\times$ reduction, confirmed against a physics-layer bypass monitor independent of the vehicles' own state estimators. **Fig. 8** shows the effect as one picture: a single matched pair of wind-forced runs (`flight_exp4_A_legacy.csv` / `flight_exp4_B_lag.csv`, identical stack, scenario and wind; only $\tau_{\mathrm{model}}$ differs) plotted as worst-agent instantaneous position-error magnitude on a log axis — the legacy model climbs to a 22 m wind-window peak while the interface-consistent controller stays at 0.10 m, a 227$\times$ separation. (This is one pair drawn for visualization; the metric here is instantaneous error, not the windowed peak-to-peak statistic of §6.2b, and the run-to-run spread of the diverging arm is documented there.)

**6.2b Repeated-trial and $\tau$-sensitivity results.** The single-run effect sizes in §6.2 were obtained from one trial per condition; we additionally ran $n\ge5$ independent repetitions of the unforced and wind-forced circular scenarios (before/after the fix) and a six-point sweep of the augmented model's assumed $\tau$, to quantify run-to-run variance and confirm the fix's effect is not an artifact of a single favourable trial. Three methodological notes apply to all repeated trials reported here: (i) this SITL stack has no fixed random-number seed governing trial-to-trial variation — variance instead arises from genuine scheduling and EKF-convergence timing nondeterminism across independent Gazebo/PX4/DDS process restarts, so these are repeated real-timing trials rather than a seeded Monte Carlo study; (ii) each repetition restarts the full simulation stack (Gazebo, all PX4 instances, the DDS bridge) rather than only the controller, since the terminal state of one trajectory would otherwise contaminate the next trial's initial condition; (iii) $p$-values throughout are reported per test without multiplicity correction — at these sample sizes ($n=3$–$5$) they serve as descriptive summaries alongside the reported effect magnitudes, not as confirmatory tests. **Statistical comparisons.** Because the wind-forced distributions are strongly right-skewed (a saturation-triggered instability whose amplitude depends on trigger timing), all group comparisons in this paper use the exact two-sided Mann–Whitney $U$ test ($n=5$ per group unless noted), reported with the Cliff's $\delta$ effect size and a $2\times10^4$-sample bootstrap 95 % confidence interval on the difference in medians; $p=0.008$ is the smallest value attainable at $n=5{,}5$, so with these sample sizes only order-of-magnitude effects (the fix, the consensus baseline) reach significance, while within-order differences (e.g. DOB vs. legacy) do not — a limitation we state explicitly rather than over-read. Around twenty pairwise comparisons are reported across §5.6, §6.2b, §6.2c, §6.2d and §6.5; we apply no multiplicity correction, and note that the one topology result that does survive — `trio3`, the smallest fleet, below every larger one at $p\le0.004$ — also survives a Bonferroni adjustment for the four topology comparisons, while the DOB and consensus comparisons are read as effect-size statements rather than as a family of tests. The one-sided tests in §5.6 are directional because $\zeta(\tau,\lambda)$ predicts the direction in advance, not because the direction was chosen after inspecting the data; that the direction was fixed beforehand is precisely what makes the sweep's failure to confirm it informative rather than merely inconclusive. The analysis script and the per-run flight logs it recomputes from are released with the code (`report/stats_significance.py`, which recomputes them from the per-run logs under `report/data/`).

Unforced circular tracking (mean steady-state pos. error, $n=5$): before the fix, $0.359\pm0.002$ m; after, $0.035\pm0.001$ m — consistent with the single-run values in §6.2 (0.368 m / 0.035 m) and showing low run-to-run variance in both conditions. Wind-forced tracking under the same $n=5$ protocol shows a starkly different variance profile. *Metric.* Unless noted otherwise, a run's wind-window peak-to-peak error is the largest single-vehicle peak-to-peak position error over the wind window — that run's **worst agent**; §6.2c reports the complementary per-agent statistic and explains when each is appropriate. Pre- and post-fix runs are reduced identically, so every before/after comparison below is like-for-like. Before the fix, wind-window peak-to-peak error has median 153 m and range [119, 816] m across the five runs (individual values: 119, 138, 153, 214, 816 m; mean $288$ m is reported for reference only — with one run at 816 m the distribution is strongly right-skewed and effectively one-sided, so a mean $\pm$ s.d. of $288\pm297$ m over-summarizes it and we rely on the median and range instead). The steady-state error shows the same skew (median 38 m, range [30, 242] m). This extreme run-to-run spread is itself the point: the divergence is not a fixed-amplitude oscillation but a saturation-triggered instability whose magnitude depends on the timing of the triggering perturbation. After the fix, mean steady-state pos. error $0.063\pm0.001$ m and peak-to-peak error $0.612\pm0.022$ m, a two-to-three-order-of-magnitude reduction with markedly tighter variance than the pre-fix condition. The single-run wind-forced values reported in §6.2 (33.14 m / 0.13 m) are **not** directly comparable with these five repetitions: they are along-wind-axis statistics averaged per vehicle, whereas the $n=5$ figures here are peak-to-peak values of the position-error *magnitude* over the wind window. They are therefore reported as a separate, differently-defined effect size rather than as a sample from the same distribution; we report both because the $n=5$ statistics establish that the qualitative result (severe divergence before the fix, tight convergence after) is not a single-trial artifact, while the original single-run values remain the historical reference point used elsewhere in this paper and in the underlying technical report.

The $\tau$-sensitivity sweep (unforced circular scenario, single run per point except the $\tau=0$ and $\tau=0.5$ endpoints which reuse the $n=5$ means above) shows steady-state pos. error falling monotonically as the augmented model's assumed $\tau$ increases from 0 toward the identified value, then plateauing: 0.359 m ($\tau=0$), 0.065 m ($\tau=0.2$), 0.046 m ($\tau=0.3$), 0.036 m ($\tau=0.4$), 0.034 m ($\tau=0.48$, the identified value), 0.035 m ($\tau=0.5$, the production default), 0.035 m ($\tau=0.6$), 0.037 m ($\tau=0.8$). The curve is essentially flat and near-minimal across $\tau\in[0.4,0.8]$ (a 0.003 m spread against a 0.325 m drop over $\tau\in[0,0.4]$), indicating the fix is insensitive to moderate over-estimation of $\tau$ around the true value — a wide enough safety margin that the single first-order fit used in this paper (§5.4), despite being methodologically simpler than the grey-box identification of [Stastny18], is adequate for this application.

**6.2c Single-vehicle seed, and a falsified coupling hypothesis.** To locate where the divergence originates — and to test the causal role of the formation-keeping weight directly rather than through the topology proxy of §5.6 — we ran two matched controlled experiments under the legacy ($\tau_{\mathrm{model}}=0$), wind-forced protocol of §6.2b ($n=5$; medians reported given the same right-skew). *A single vehicle already diverges:* solo1 reaches a wind-window peak-to-peak of median 49 m (range 45–55 m; steady-state error median 8.7 m), which the interface-consistent fix ($\tau=0.5$) collapses to 0.24 m peak-to-peak (steady-state 0.045 m) — the identical mechanism and remedy as in the formation, operating on one vehicle. *Five vehicles roughly double it, but not through the explicit coupling* (Table II-b): five vehicles with the formation weight set to zero ($w_f=0$) — decoupled in the *explicit* formation cost but still sharing a common leader reference, a common world frame, and a pairwise collision term — diverge to median 125 m, roughly 2.5$\times$ the single-vehicle seed; restoring and increasing the explicit weight ($w_f=0.25/0.5/1.0/2.0$) raises the worst-agent median to 112 / 153 / 169 / 144 m, a further $\approx$1.2–1.4$\times$. **That last figure, however, is metric-dependent and we report it as such.** The two statistics answer different questions: the *worst-agent* value governs formation integrity, while the *per-agent* value (the mean over vehicles of each vehicle's peak-to-peak) is the one that can be compared across fleet sizes without an order-statistic bias — the maximum over five vehicles exceeds the maximum over one even for identical per-vehicle distributions. Under the per-agent statistic the same eight-fold change in $w_f$ (0 $\to$ 2.0) leaves the typical vehicle essentially at $\approx$2.1$\times$ the seed throughout (per-agent medians 88.7–104.4 m across the sweep, non-monotonically: $w_f$=0.25 sits *below* $w_f$=0), whereas the implicit couplings alone already deliver 2.1$\times$ — and the null model below delivers the full amplification with no coupling at all. What the explicit formation weight mainly does, therefore, is widen the *spread across vehicles* — which matters operationally, since formation integrity is set by the worst agent — rather than worsen the typical vehicle. Neither explicit coupling term, then, accounts for the amplification. To close the decomposition we removed **both** ($w_f=0$ *and* $w_c=0$, leaving only a shared leader reference and a common world frame): the divergence is essentially unchanged on both statistics (worst-agent median 131 m against 125 m at $w_f=0$; per-agent median 96.9 m against 102 m), and still significantly above the single vehicle ($p=0.008$ on the per-agent statistic, with no overlap between the solo1 range $[45,55]$ m and the decoupled five-vehicle range $[91,130]$ m). A matched single-vehicle comparison sharpens this: `cross5`'s vehicle 0 carries a zero formation offset, so its reference is *pointwise identical* to solo1's, yet with all coupling removed it still diverges to 80.7 m against solo1's 49.0 m ($p=0.008$) — the only difference being that four other vehicles exist in the same simulation. Pre-wind steady-state error for that same vehicle is if anything *lower* in the five-vehicle case (0.144 vs 0.163 m), which argues against a compute-load explanation. Which shared element carries this residual effect — the reference protocol or the multi-vehicle simulation environment itself — is answered by the null model below: removing the shared reference as well changes nothing, so it is the environment.

**A five-independent-solo null model closes the decomposition.** We ran the null model pre-registered in §7.2: five vehicles in one simulation, each tracking its *own* independent leader on its own circle, with the neighbour set empty (formation and collision terms structurally inactive in the OCP), the shared altitude datum disabled, and every swarm topic remapped per vehicle — the same spawn geometry, reference trajectories, wind protocol and host load as the coupled case, with zero inter-vehicle information flow and no shared reference. Because this experiment ran on a second simulation host whose solver build is not numerically comparable to the primary one (§6.6 documents the same caution for hardware), all three arms — solo, null model, and the coupled $w_f{=}0$ case — were re-flown there together under the identical protocol (the single-vehicle median on that host is 80.4 m rather than 49.0 m, which is exactly why only same-host ratios are compared; one null-model run was discarded whole by the validity screen, leaving $n=4$ vs. 5 vs. 5). The result (Fig. 9): the zero-coupling null model diverges to a per-agent median of 125.8 m against the coupled case's 125.1 m — a ratio of 1.01 ($p=0.41$, Cliff's $\delta=+0.40$; medians 1.6$\times$ the same-host solo) — and every null-model run exceeds every same-host single-vehicle run ($\delta=+1.00$, $p=0.016$, the smallest attainable at $n=4{,}5$). The five-vehicle amplification is reproduced *in full* with no inter-vehicle coupling of any kind and no shared reference; what carries it must be the shared scenario itself — five lag-blind planners flown concurrently on one host under one wind field — though which scenario factor (concurrent computational load, common forcing timing, physical proximity) dominates we leave open.

**6.2d Is the interface mismatch simply a matter of inner-loop tuning?** A natural objection to the diagnosis is that $\tau\approx0.48$ s reflects an *under-tuned* PX4 velocity loop rather than a property of the interface itself. The objection has a concrete basis: PX4's default horizontal velocity gain is `MPC_XY_VEL_P_ACC`$=1.8$, for which the documented first-order relation gives $\tau\approx1/1.8\approx0.55$ s, and the comparison in §3.3 against [NigusieAST26]'s $\tau=0.03$–$0.08$ s invites exactly that reading. (That relation is not merely documented but measured on our own hardware: §6.6 identifies $\tau=0.550$–$0.585$ s on the horizontal loop at $K=1.8$ and $0.27$–$0.36$ s on the vertical loop at $K=4.0$, matching $1/K$ to within 1 % on the former.) If tuning the inner loop faster removed the failure, the practical implication of §7.3 would be "tune your inner loop", not "model your interface". We therefore tested it directly, under the legacy ($\tau_{\mathrm{model}}=0$), wind-forced `cross5` protocol of §6.2b, raising the PX4 velocity-loop gain to $2\times$ and $3\times$ the default (`MPC_XY_VEL_P_ACC` $=3.6$ and $5.4$, each verified in the per-instance boot log), $n=3$ per setting.

*Manipulation check — the gain does what it should.* Pre-wind steady-state tracking error falls monotonically with gain: 0.149 m at 1.8, 0.114 m at 3.6, and 0.092 m at 5.4 — a 38 % reduction, significant at both settings ($p=0.036$, the smallest value attainable at $n=3,5$) — while the commanded speed is unchanged (1.016 / 1.014 / 1.012 m/s), i.e. the *same* trajectory is being flown by a measurably faster loop.

*Null result — the divergence is untouched.* Wind-forced per-vehicle peak-to-peak error is 101.4 m (gain 1.8), 89.9 m (3.6) and 95.4 m (5.4): no statistically detectable change ($p=0.39$ and $p=0.57$ against the default; Cliff's $\delta=+0.47$ and $+0.33$ — moderate point estimates in the direction of lower divergence at higher gain, quantifying exactly the modest effect the next sentence concedes cannot be excluded). We state plainly what this test can and cannot do: at $n=3$ against $n=5$ it would reject only under near-complete separation of the two samples, so it cannot exclude a modest effect, and the substantive claim rests not on these $p$-values but on the magnitudes — across all eleven runs the divergence stays within 78–255 m, i.e. 128–416$\times$ the interface-consistent fix's 0.61 m, so no gain setting brings the legacy controller within two orders of magnitude of the fix.

*Reading.* Tripling the inner-loop gain measurably improves nominal tracking and does nothing to the saturation-driven divergence, because the MPC's model asserts $\tau=0$ either way: whether the true lag is 0.55 s or 0.19 s, the internal model is wrong by 100 %. What matters is the *structural* mismatch, not its magnitude — the same conclusion the fix's $\tau$-insensitivity over $[0.4,0.8]$ s reaches from the other direction (§6.2b). One caveat: we did not re-identify $\tau$ at the new gains, since the 1 Hz telemetry is far too coarse for a $\approx0.2$ s time constant; the quoted $\tau$ values follow PX4's documented $\tau\approx1/$`MPC_XY_VEL_P_ACC` relation, and the manipulation check above is our evidence that the loop did in fact become faster. Data are released in `report/data/review_2026_08_17/` with the analysis in `report/analyze_review_batch.py`.

**6.3 Full regression.** Enabling the fix across a 41-scenario regression suite (scenario IDs `S0`–`S34`, several IDs carrying more than one variant; covering formation sizes one to nine vehicles, line/circle/hover leader trajectories, perturbed initial conditions, node-kill and communication-dropout fault injection, and safety-filter-induced degradation) yields 41/41 scenarios passing: 36 pass on the CSV thresholds — 35 directly and one (`S15_cross5_conservative`) under a recalibrated recording window, see below — while the remaining 5, all safety-filter scenarios whose intended behaviour is a commanded deviation from the tracking reference, are adjudicated on the safety-event log instead. **6.4 An independent defect uncovered during regression.** Three of the five *fault-injection* scenarios — the three node-kill cases; these are distinct from the five safety-filter LOG-SEEN rows of Table II — shared a common cause unrelated to the velocity-lag fix: when one agent's MPC node fell silent (simulated node failure or a startup arming race) while its flight-control unit continued to publish stale-but-fresh position telemetry, the *surviving* agents' formation-keeping cost continued to pull them toward that agent's position, producing a constant tracking bias (up to 1.6 m) or, under a rotating leader trajectory, a periodic oscillation synchronized with the formation's rotation. We traced this to the formation term never becoming inactive for a neighbour whose predicted-trajectory broadcast had timed out but whose raw position telemetry remained fresh, and fixed it by dynamically neutralizing the formation term's reference distance to the agent's *current* actual distance whenever this condition is detected, leaving the hard collision-avoidance floor unaffected. This defect and its fix are independent of the velocity-lag interface mismatch that is the focus of this paper, but were only surfaced by the regression required to validate the fix in §6.1–6.3, and we report it for completeness.

To test whether the divergence is a generic consequence of coupling a lagged velocity plant through a persistent formation objective — as opposed to something specific to *model-predictive* control — we replace the MPC with a memoryless distributed displacement-consensus law, $v_{\mathrm{cmd},i}=v_{\ell}+k_L\,(p_\ell+\delta_i-p_i)+k_C\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}\big((p_j+\delta_i-\delta_j)-p_i\big)$, driven through the *identical* PX4 velocity loop, wind protocol, and `cross5` scenario as rows (a)/(d). Thus even under deeper saturation than the one that destabilizes legacy MPC, the memoryless law does not enter the divergent limit cycle. **We read this as localizing the pathology to the predictive controller's internal lag-blind model** — not to the formation coupling, the plant, or velocity saturation per se — which is consistent with, and mechanistically explains, why the principled remedy corrects that internal model (§6.1) rather than adding an external disturbance term (§3.3, row (c)). **Fig. 7:** (a) worst-agent tracking error over time under wind — legacy diverges while both consensus gains stay bounded; (b) raising the consensus gain deepens velocity saturation $\approx$11$\times$ (peak commanded speed $0.9\times\to10.7\times$ the limit) while the wind-forced error barely moves ($0.89\to1.54$ m), whereas legacy diverges at only $\approx$2.7$\times$ saturation.

**DOB-MPC baseline (completed) — filling rows (c)/(c$'$) of Table III:**

We implemented the FxTDO-family observer specified for this falsification and ran it in closed loop. **Observer:** a no-integrator Q-filter disturbance observer (standard Ohishi form, $\hat f_d=\mathrm{LPF}_L[\dot v_{\mathrm{meas}}-u]$ with cutoff $L=1$ rad/s — deliberately low-bandwidth, i.e. quasi-static per [Xu24, Assumption 1]), estimating a lumped translational $\hat f_d\in\mathbb R^3$ from the commanded-vs-realized velocity residual and fed forward into the *same* zero-lag OCP as row (a) via $\dot v=u+\hat f_d$ (added to the legacy model, not to the interface-consistent one); it engages only after formation acquisition so that start-up is identical to legacy. **Protocol:** the identical `cross5`/`trio3` circular wind-forcing of §6.2b, $n=5$, medians reported. **Result — the §3.3 prediction is confirmed:** with a conservative estimate cap (0.5 m/s²) the wind-forced peak-to-peak error is median 125 m (`cross5`, range [0.37, 188] m) / 86.5 m (`trio3`, range [66, 105] m) — statistically indistinguishable from legacy row (a) (153 / 90.7 m; two-sided Mann–Whitney $p=0.15$ for `cross5`, $p=0.42$ for `trio3`) and two orders of magnitude above the interface-consistent fix (0.61 m). The additive observer does not touch the instability. We report the `cross5` range untrimmed: one of those five runs stayed at 0.37 m and never entered the divergence at all. That is the same trigger-timing lottery which gives the legacy condition its [119, 816] m spread, and it is the single most favourable observation the observer receives anywhere in this study — discarding it would only strengthen the negative result, so we keep it. **Raising the observer's authority (cap 2.0 m/s²) does not rescue it either, and the mechanism explains why it cannot.** At the aggressive cap the median is 168 m (`cross5`), but at this variance that is *not* a statistically distinguishable change from legacy (two-sided Mann–Whitney $p=0.55$, Cliff's $\delta=+0.28$, bootstrap 95 % CI on the median difference $[-648,+369]$ m; the aggressive-DOB *mean*, 236 m, is in fact below legacy's 288 m, which a single 816 m legacy run inflates). We therefore do *not* claim the aggressive observer empirically worsens the divergence — at $n=5$ it neither reliably helps nor harms. The informative result is structural: because the velocity loop attenuates the realized acceleration to $\dot v\approx0.1\,u$, the observer's residual is $\dot v-u\approx-0.9\,u$ — a signal *correlated with the command itself*, not an exogenous disturbance. Feeding it back as $\dot v=u+\hat f_d$ therefore acts as positive feedback (loop gain $\sim1/0.1$) that can only push toward, never away from, instability; a tight cap throttles it back toward the legacy response, a loose cap unleashes it. This is the clearest confirmation of N2: an additive DOB estimates the command, not a disturbance, so it is the wrong tool for an input-channel lag at *any* tuning — the point is its structural inapplicability, not an empirical worsening. With this, every row of Table III is built from data already in the repository; the remaining experimental gap — a *controlled* before/after comparison on real hardware — is bounded in §6.6 and discussed in §7.2.




### 6.6 A Real-Flight Datapoint, and What It Does Not Establish

**Setup.** After the SITL study we deployed the interface-consistent controller of §6.1 — the same MPC node, the same acados solver, the same $\tau_{\mathrm{model}}=0.5$ s — to hardware: a ZeroOne X6 Air+ quadrotor running PX4 v1.16, with an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX companion computer solving the OCP on board and publishing velocity setpoints over micro-XRCE-DDS, flying outdoors under RTK GNSS (RTK-Fixed throughout, reported horizontal standard deviation $\approx$1 cm). The flights are single-vehicle (`solo1`); the pilot took off manually and handed control to OFFBOARD in the air. All numbers below are recomputed from the archived logs by `report/realflight_provenance.py` (first campaign) and `report/analyze_solo_20260831.py` (second campaign); the logs themselves — $\approx$250 MB of 1 Hz diagnostic CSV, 100 Hz rosbag, and the flight controller's onboard logs — are archived outside the repository for size, and are available from the authors on request; a DOI-referenced archive will accompany final publication. This section reports three things, in order: a deployability and real-time-feasibility datapoint together with what it does not establish; a hardware identification of the plant lag on both control loops; and two plant-side effects that SITL does not reproduce.

**Result: the controller flies, in real time, to centimetre-level tracking.** In a 36 s OFFBOARD hover, the settled window (from 15 s after handover; $n=22$ at 1 Hz) gives a horizontal position error of mean 0.032 m, max 0.057 m, and an altitude error of mean 0.073 m. In a circular scenario the leader reference is a circle of radius 3.0000 m (algebraic fit residual s.d. 0.2 mm) traversed for one complete revolution — 358.4° in 26 s, of which 342.9° at the nominal 0.80 m/s outside the speed ramp. Against it the vehicle held a tracking error of mean 0.039 m, median 0.036 m, max 0.083 m ($n=24$), a realized radius of $2.990\pm0.042$ m and an altitude of $-3.016\pm0.026$ m, with **zero controller fallbacks in these flights, zero safety violations, and a worst-case solve time of 7.14 ms** (mean 3.65 ms) against the 20 ms budget of the 50 Hz control loop.

**What this does *not* establish.** It is a deployability and real-time-feasibility datapoint, not a hardware confirmation of the fix. The controlled comparison that carries the argument in SITL — legacy against interface-consistent, everything else matched (§6.2) — has no hardware counterpart here, because its legacy arm would mean deliberately flying the diverging controller on a real airframe. We therefore claim only that the corrected controller performs well on hardware, and *not* that the fix has been validated there; the SITL before/after comparisons remain the sole evidence for the fix itself. The flights are also single-vehicle, so nothing in them speaks to the formation-coupling questions of §5.5 and §6.2c.

**The plant lag on hardware, measured on both loops.** The flight controller's own high-rate logs were subsequently retrieved over the MAVLink log-download interface, and they contain one 133.5 s OFFBOARD circle with sustained lateral excitation. Fitting the first-order model of §5.4 to it by *free simulation* — driven only by the command sequence and an initial condition, never by the measured state — gives $\tau=0.585$ s on the north axis ($R^2=0.985$, residual r.m.s. 0.031 m/s) and $\tau=0.550$ s on the east axis ($R^2=0.982$, 0.033 m/s). A pure-delay exchange check leaves the total lag nearly invariant: sweeping an inserted transport delay $T_d$ from 0 to 0.20 s moves $\tau$ down in step, with $\tau+T_d$ staying within 0.585–0.600 s (north) and 0.550–0.570 s (east). The magnitude is therefore set by the data and not by the modelling choice of which element absorbs the lag. The vertical loop, identified from a separate flight, gives $\tau=0.33$–$0.36$ s by the ODE-residual fit ($R^2\ge0.976$) and $0.27$–$0.31$ s by a discrete first-order ARX fit.

Both loops corroborate the first-order relation $\tau\approx1/K$ invoked in §6.2d, and the horizontal one does so sharply. The airframe's own parameter snapshot has `MPC_XY_VEL_P_ACC`$=1.8$ and `MPC_Z_VEL_P_ACC`$=4.0$, for which the relation predicts 0.556 s and 0.250 s; we measure 0.550–0.585 s and 0.27–0.36 s. The horizontal prediction is met to within 1 %, and the relation holds across two loops whose gains differ by a factor of 2.2 — the analysis of §6.2d, which asks whether the reported effect is merely inner-loop tuning, rests on exactly this relation. **The measured horizontal $\tau$ lies above the 0.48 s identified in SITL**, confirming the direction of what the previous version of this section could only offer as an extrapolation: on this airframe the hardware velocity loop is slower than its simulated counterpart, not faster.

**Two caveats bound the horizontal figure.** First, the identification is weakly conditioned. A 3 m circle flown at 0.8 m/s excites the loop near 0.03 Hz, an order of magnitude below the model's own bandwidth $1/2\pi\tau\approx0.29$ Hz; only 1.3 % (north) and 12.7 % (east) of the commanded-velocity power lies above that bandwidth. A shorter record (25.6 s) returns $\tau=1.03$ s and $0.755$ s on the two axes — a spread of roughly a factor of two against the values above. We take the long record as the primary estimate because it is five times longer and fits better ($R^2$ 0.982–0.985 against 0.953–0.967), but the spread is real, and it is a consequence of the excitation rather than of the model: a commanded-velocity *step*, instead of the trapezoidal and sinusoidal profiles flown here, would place power at the bandwidth and collapse it. Second, the "measured" velocity is the EKF estimate, which itself lags the true velocity; these figures are therefore upper bounds.

A methodological note, because the trap is easy to fall into: on the same records a one-step-ahead fit returns $R^2=0.9999$ for essentially any $\tau$ over a wide range. At 50 Hz the model advances the state by $dt/\tau\approx4\%$ per step, so nearly all of the variance is explained by the previous sample whatever the parameter is. One-step prediction is not a criterion here; every figure above is a free-simulation fit.

**A second campaign: the error equation replicates, and the plant-side disturbance scales with ground proximity.** A later flight day (three scenarios — hover, straight line, and the same 3 m circle — each ending in an autonomous mission-complete landing) adds two datapoints worth reporting. First, the hover altitude error is again predicted by the stationary-error relation of the pure-P vertical loop: the EKF vertical-velocity bias measured $-0.096$ m/s and the altitude error $0.090$ m against the predicted $\mathrm{bias}/K_p=0.096$ m — the first campaign had matched the same relation at $0.058\to0.057$ m. Second, both the bias and the barometric-height innovation grew as the hover altitude was lowered from 4 m to 3 m (innovation $-1.02\to-1.58$ m), the direction expected if rotor downwash disturbing the static-pressure port is the underlying mechanism, since the disturbance should strengthen with ground proximity; wind was not instrumented on the second day, so we report this as consistent with, not proof of, that mechanism. The circle radius reproduced to a millimetre ($2.989\pm0.022$ m against $2.990\pm0.042$ m) at unchanged solve times. One operational observation belongs here because it exercised a code path no earlier flight had: on two flights OFFBOARD was engaged about 1.5 m below the reference altitude, and the first OCP solves failed for roughly one second (7–8 consecutive solver failures) before recovering — during which the controller's hover fallback held the vehicle, exactly as designed. The fallback path, previously validated only in SITL, has now fired and recovered in real flight.

**A failure mode that SITL does not reproduce.** On one flight OFFBOARD was engaged with the vehicle still on the ground. The controller commanded a 0.8 m/s climb for 6.6 s while the vehicle did not move — position error, and with it the commanded velocity, accumulating against the ground contact — and on break-away the realized climb rate overshot to 2.72 m/s, **340 % of the commanded rate**, rising 1.12 m before the pilot intervened. (On a second flight, engaged at 0.8 m altitude, the same effect appears in miniature: 0.8 s static, 109 % overshoot.) We were unable to reproduce this in Gazebo, where the simulated vehicle is dragged along the ground rather than restrained by it. The episode is worth reporting for two reasons. It is the same command-versus-realization gap this paper is about, arising here from a *plant* feature — ground contact — rather than from the velocity loop; and it is a concrete instance of the boundary drawn in §4, where we argued that SITL reproduces the *structure* of the interface but substitutes the plant around it. The structure transferred to hardware exactly as §4 predicts; a plant-side effect did not.


## 7. Discussion



**7.1 Why this is a diagnosis, not a method, contribution.** As established in §3.2, augmenting an identified low-level lag into an MPC's prediction model is not new; nor is the observation that PX4's velocity loop lags. The contribution of this paper is showing what happens when a receding-horizon planner is *wrong* about that lag rather than merely subject to it — a saturation-driven instability rather than a tracking penalty, one that low-level retuning does not remove — quantifying it, ruling out the more commonly reached-for explanation (an exogenous disturbance) both empirically and structurally, and validating a fix drawn directly from the existing single-vehicle literature. We also report a hypothesis of our own that we could not sustain: that the persistent formation-keeping cost is what amplifies the mismatch across a swarm (§5.5, §6.2c). We consider this framing more defensible than a "new method" claim, which — as §3.2 shows in detail — would not survive comparison with [Kamel16] or [Stastny18].

**7.2 Limitations.** All validation reported here is SITL-only (PX4 software-in-the-loop with Gazebo physics), and while PX4 SITL is built from the same flight-control source tree as a hardware build — so the *structure* under study is that of a genuine flight-control stack rather than a purely synthetic model (§4), albeit at a different release (v1.14 here against v1.16 on the target airframes; §4) — the only real-flight evidence is the single-vehicle deployability datapoint of §6.6, which carries no legacy arm and therefore validates no comparison; the *numerical* value $\tau=0.48$ s should be read as specific to this airframe and plant model rather than as a property of PX4 in general — indeed §6.6 shows that even the hardware lag we could measure belongs to a different (vertical) velocity loop. Our own $\tau$-identification procedure is a single first-order fit from one measurement pass, methodologically simpler than the formal grey-box system-identification pipeline of [Stastny18] (§3.2); §6.2b reports a $\tau$-sensitivity sweep and $n\ge5$ repeated-trial statistics under this stack's inherent timing nondeterminism (no fixed random seed governs EKF convergence timing or scheduler jitter), which together show the fix is insensitive to moderate $\tau$ mis-identification and that its effect is not a single-trial artifact — but we have not repeated the $\tau$-identification procedure itself across multiple independent 130 Hz measurement passes to quantify identification-run variance, only the downstream tracking-error effect of assuming different $\tau$ values. Line, hover, and large-perturbation scenarios have not been separately profiled for this specific interaction, though they are covered by the 41-scenario regression of §6.3 for the fix's absence of side effects. A controlled sweep ($n=5$ per topology, matched leader speed, duration, collision weight and build, §5.6) found the sparsest topology significantly least affected but did *not* recover the ordering $\zeta(\tau,\lambda)$ predicts: the two five-vehicle graphs come out transposed, the fleet-size-matched pair resolves in neither direction at $n=5$, and severity instead tracks fleet size — so we report $\zeta$ and Proposition 2 as design conditions rather than as validated predictions (§5.6). An audit of the coupling measure itself explains part of that failure and bounds what the sweep could ever have shown: under the formation's rigidity spectrum rather than the plain graph Laplacian, three of the four graphs carry identical coupling stiffness ($\mu_{\max}=3.00$) and the fourth is the lowest ($2.50$), leaving a predicted spread in $\zeta$ of under 1 %, so the four formations tested were never capable of separating the condition — and, more consequentially, the densest graph is certified under $\mu$ but not under $\lambda$ — a future test must be designed around $\mu$ and on shapes chosen to spread it (§5.6); an earlier unmatched-speed exploratory pass had not even recovered the trend, underscoring the importance of controlling leader speed across topologies. A direct quantitative comparison against a DOB-MPC baseline under matched conditions has been run (§6.5) and confirms the negative result (additive compensation leaves the divergence statistically intact at any observer tuning). A further gap concerns **heterogeneous lag across vehicles**: every vehicle here runs the same firmware and tuning, so a single fleet-wide $\tau$ is assumed, whereas a realistic formation (mixed airframes, differing payloads or tuning) would present a per-vehicle $\tau$. This is precisely the desynchronization anticipated in §5.5 — a state-augmented model whose lag can drift from the plant's true lag — and it is the most obvious next failure mode for a *formation*; we have not tested it, and an interface-consistent controller carrying the wrong neighbour lag may degrade in ways this study cannot predict. Three further limitations bound the scope of our claims. **(i) Single-system evidence.** All results come from one MPC formulation, one set of cost weights, one solver, and one firmware version (PX4 v1.14); the most direct way to narrow this particular gap needs no hardware at all — repeating the key comparisons in SITL under a second firmware release would test whether the diagnosis survives a version change, and is the more pressing for us because board support for our target flight controller entered PX4 only at v1.16, so the vehicles we intend to fly can never run the version studied here (§4); although we phrase the phenomenon in the general language of distributed formation MPC, our evidence is a single-system case study, and the general claim should be read as a hypothesis supported within — not established beyond — this system. Within this system, however, a non-predictive control baseline (memoryless consensus, §6.5) run through the identical plant and coupling does *not* reproduce the divergence at any gain we tested, which localizes the effect to the predictive controller's internal model rather than to the plant or the coupling; the open generalization question is therefore across MPC formulations, solvers, and weight families (and to real hardware), not whether coupling-plus-lag alone suffices — it does not. The topology sweep (§5.6) and the completed coupling decomposition (§5.5, §6.2c) vary coupling within the system — showing the divergence persists with every explicit inter-vehicle coupling removed, and that a non-predictive consensus law does not reproduce it (§6.5) — but we have not varied the MPC formulation, solver, or weight family. A further limitation is that we could not resolve the origin of the residual five-vehicle-vs-single-vehicle difference (§6.2c): a shared leader reference and the multi-vehicle simulation environment remain confounded, and separating them would require a fleet of vehicles tracking mutually independent references. **(ii) A first-order lag model, characterized only in still air.** The adopted $\tau=0.48$ s is identified from the pre-wind window, where the first-order model reconstructs the realized velocity to $R^2=0.999$ (RMSE $\approx0.02$ m/s) and the free-fit estimate is stable to within $0.49$–$0.65$ s (median 0.55 s, s.d. 0.07 s across windows and axes, §5.4). Under wind $\tau$ is *not* separately identifiable: the diverging dynamics dominate the commanded-vs-realized relationship and the first-order fit becomes ill-conditioned (free-fit $\tau$ ranges from negative to several seconds, ODE-residual $R^2\approx0$), so the earlier impression that $\tau$ "lengthens" under wind is an identification artifact of the divergence rather than a change in the physical loop constant; we accordingly report a still-air $\tau$ only, and note that the adopted value still reconstructs the wind-forced velocity to $R^2\ge0.96$. What we have *not* done is a full grey-box identification in the manner of [Stastny18] (multiple parameters, a train/validation/test split, explicit actuator sub-models) or a repetition across independent measurement passes; the still-air fit, while well-conditioned, remains a single first-order approximation. That the fix is nonetheless effective and $\tau$-insensitive across $[0.4,0.8]$ s (§6.2b) indicates this approximation is adequate for *correcting* the mismatch even where it is imperfect as a *model*. **(iii) No controlled hardware comparison.** The minimal real-flight datapoint is now present, but one-armed: §6.6 reports a single vehicle flying the *corrected* controller to centimetre-level tracking, with no legacy counterpart, since that arm would mean deliberately flying the diverging controller on a real airframe. SITL runs the same flight-control firmware, but Gazebo physics and the SITL scheduler are not the plant, and we cannot at present exclude that some quantitative details (particularly the magnitude of the wind-forced divergence) are stack-specific — §6.6 in fact exhibits one plant-side effect, ground-contact windup at OFFBOARD engagement, that SITL does not reproduce at all. One of the two hardware measurements we previously flagged as most valuable has since been made: the horizontal lag constant is now identified from the airframe's own logs at $\tau=0.550$–$0.585$ s, above the SITL value and within 1 % of the $1/K$ relation (§6.6). It remains weakly conditioned — the flown trajectories place almost all of their command power an order of magnitude below the loop's bandwidth, and a shorter record disagrees by roughly a factor of two — so a step-excited segment would still be worth flying. The second, a formation-scale flight, remains outstanding and is the more consequential of the two: every multi-vehicle claim in this paper is SITL-only. Of the two SITL experiments previously queued here, the five-independent-solo null model has since been run and is reported in §6.2c: it resolved the amplification as a scenario effect. The second remains outstanding: a Dryden-turbulence condition to test whether the divergence and the fix carry over from the constant-force wind used throughout.

**7.3 Practical implications.** Designers of distributed formation-MPC systems should verify whether the time constant of the underlying low-level execution interface is implicitly assumed to be zero by the upper-level planner — the check matters even for a single vehicle, since the mismatch is already destabilizing there (§6.2c), and a multi-vehicle deployment amplifies the resulting divergence further — an amplification the null model of §6.2c shows to be a property of the multi-vehicle scenario rather than of the coupling terms, so no coupling-side redesign will remove it; the interface-side fix is the remedy at every fleet size. This check is inexpensive relative to the failure it can prevent: it requires only a bypass measurement of commanded-vs-realized velocity and, if a lag is found, the state-augmentation technique already established in the single-vehicle literature. It is worth stressing what the check is *not*: retuning the low-level loop is not a substitute. Tripling this stack's velocity-loop gain improves nominal tracking by 38 % yet leaves the wind-forced divergence statistically unchanged (§6.2d), because a planner that assumes zero lag is wrong by 100 % whatever the true lag happens to be.



## 8. Conclusion



In a single-system SITL case study of a PX4-based distributed MPC swarm (one-to-nine vehicles), we diagnosed a failure mode of the planner/execution interface: a well-known, individually-documented velocity-loop lag in PX4's low-level control stack which, because the planner's model asserts it to be zero, produces not degraded tracking but a saturation-driven instability (a saturation-bounded limit cycle unforced, a divergence under wind) — and one that retuning the low-level loop does not remove. We argued this failure mode to be ill-suited to additive disturbance-observer compensation — and mechanistically distinct from the exogenous disturbances targeted by disturbance-observer-based MPC — and resolved it in SITL by adopting, unmodified, an established single-vehicle lag-augmentation technique, verified across a 41-scenario regression suite and supported by $n\ge5$ repeated-trial statistics and a $\tau$-sensitivity sweep. A direct closed-loop DOB-MPC baseline comparison, a $w_f$-sweep test of the amplification claim, and a matched-protocol topology sweep have since been completed (§6.5, §6.2c, §5.6) — the first confirming that additive compensation leaves the divergence statistically intact at any observer tuning (the residual it estimates is the command, not a disturbance); the second locating the divergence's single-vehicle seed and falsifying our own coupling hypothesis; the third declining to recover the severity ordering our design condition implies, with severity tracking fleet size instead. We report both negative results as they stand: the diagnosis and the fix rest on the controlled before/after comparisons, not on either of them. Whether the mechanism and its remedy transfer across MPC formulations, solvers, and weight families — and, above all, to real hardware — remains a conjecture that this single-system study supports but does not establish. A first hardware datapoint has since been obtained (§6.6): the corrected controller flies a real quadrotor under RTK GNSS around a 3 m circle at a 0.039 m mean tracking error, in real time and without a single solver fallback. It carries no legacy arm, so it establishes deployability rather than validating the fix, and the lag it lets us measure turns out to belong to PX4's vertical rather than horizontal velocity loop; a controlled hardware comparison, and an in-flight identification of the horizontal lag, remain the principal items of future work.

## Appendix B. Derivations and Asides for §5.6

This appendix collects the derivation-level material referenced from §5.6: the bookkeeping that extracts $K_p$ and $K_v$ from the implemented OCP and verifies they are the controller's own, the legacy loop's closed form for contrast, the role of assumption A2 in the certificate, and the relations to classical delay-margin theory and to a robust-control reading. The propositions themselves, their numerical validation, and the topology sweep remain in the main text.

**Where the gains come from, and a check that they are the controller's.** Everything below rests on $K_p$ and $K_v$ being the feedback the MPC actually applies, so we compute them from the implemented problem rather than assume a textbook form (`report/verify_lqr_equivalence.py`, `report/verify_permode_model.py`; matrix recursions, no simulation and no solver call). Two facts make the computation exact. First, in the regime where this section applies — collision hinge inactive, box constraints inactive, formation cost linearized — the OCP of §4 is exactly linear-quadratic, so the feedback the solver returns at the first stage is given in closed form by a backward Riccati recursion over the *implemented* horizon ($N_h=30$), shooting interval ($\Delta t=0.05$ s) and terminal weight ($2q_p$). Second, the implementation penalizes the *realized acceleration* rather than the decision variable — for the interface-consistent model the decision variable is the velocity setpoint and $a=(u-v)/\tau$, for the legacy model $a=u$ — while the dynamics are $\dot x=(v,a)$ in both cases. The two controllers therefore solve the *same* LQ problem in the acceleration variable, and command the same acceleration; they differ only in whether the plant realizes it.

At the identified $\tau=0.48$ s and with no coupling, the recursion returns $K_p=2.53$ s$^{-1}$ and $K_v=1.46$. Two cross-checks confirm these are the right objects. **(i) Acceleration equivalence.** Re-expressed as an acceleration law the same feedback reads $\ddot e=-(K_p/\tau)e-((1+K_v)/\tau)\dot e = -5.28\,e-5.12\,\dot e$, within $0.4\,\%$ and $1.4\,\%$ of the $5.26$ and $5.05$ the legacy double-integrator problem returns — as it must be, since the two are the same LQ problem in $a$. **Proposition 1's premise — that the per-mode error dynamics above are the interface-consistent controller's closed loop — is therefore verified rather than assumed.** **(ii) Distance from the textbook closed form.** The continuous infinite-horizon LQR of the same stage cost gives the familiar $k_p=\sqrt{q_p/r_a}=6.32$ s$^{-2}$ and $k_v=\sqrt{(2\sqrt{r_aq_p}+q_v)/r_a}=5.71$ s$^{-1}$, which overstate the implemented acceleration gains by $17\,\%$ and $12\,\%$. The gap is dominated not by the finite horizon but by the $\Delta t=0.05$ s shooting interval (the controller re-solves at 50 Hz but discretizes at 20 Hz): the closed loop's fast pole has a $0.077$ s time constant, so one shooting interval is $0.65$ of it and the sampled optimum genuinely differs from the continuous one ($-13.3\,\%$/$-10.8\,\%$), the horizon adding only a further $-4.2\,\%$/$-1.1\,\%$ and the gains having converged by $N_h=50$. The terminal weight is nearly irrelevant — sweeping $q_p^{\mathrm{term}}/q_p$ from $0$ to $100$ moves $k_p$ only from $5.19$ to $5.70$ — worth stating plainly, since a terminal cost of that size is a tracking heuristic and not a terminal ingredient in the stability-theoretic sense (honesty note (ii) below). We quote the closed form only as the practitioner's rule of thumb; every number in this section is computed from the implemented problem.

**The legacy loop, for contrast.** A3 excludes the legacy controller from the analysis, but the same recursion prices it in one line and settles a claim made in the abstract. Legacy publishes the *predicted velocity* one shooting interval ahead, $u=v+a_0\Delta t$, so the plant realizes $\dot v=(u-v)/\tau=a_0\Delta t/\tau$: only $\Delta t/\tau=10.4\,\%$ of the commanded acceleration — the "order of magnitude" of over-estimated acceleration authority, now derived rather than asserted. The resulting small-signal loop is $\ddot e+0.53\dot e+0.55e=0$: **underdamped at $\zeta=0.36$, against $1.11$ for the fix**. It is nevertheless still stable, so — consistent with what this section has said throughout — the divergence remains something no linear model of this loop accounts for. (Nor should the $8.5$ s natural period be matched against the observed oscillation: §5.4 reports that both its amplitude *and* its period scale with `max_speed`, which is the signature of a saturation-driven limit cycle, not of this linear mode.)

**This is where assumption A2 decides the outcome, not merely the presentation.** Under the superseded plain-Laplacian proxy the same computation gives $0.69/0.56/0.54/0.49/0.46$ s — and the last of these is *below* the identified lag: **`grid9` fails the certificate under $\lambda$ and passes under $\mu$** ($K_v-\tau K_p=-0.022$ against $+0.099$). A coupling measure that overstates the densest graph by a factor of two is therefore not a presentational detail; it is the difference between a certified and an uncertified configuration, which is why the audit after the sweep is reported as a result rather than a caveat. The design trade-off is otherwise unchanged and now certified: admissible lag shrinks as coupling stiffness $\nu$ or formation weight $w_f$ grows, and for a heterogeneous fleet it is the *largest* per-vehicle $\tau$ that binds — a claim the lag-invariance argument above now supports rather than merely asserts (§7.2).

Three honesty notes accompany this result. (i) It is *sufficient, not necessary*, and — as the numerical check below quantifies — very conservative: over a wide $(\tau,\nu)$ grid the saturated per-mode loop converges everywhere, including at points the certificate rejects, so violating the condition does not imply instability. (ii) It certifies the *static-equivalent* loop of the interface-consistent controller under the simplifying assumptions A1--A3 above, not the receding-horizon controller with its full constraint set; a guarantee for the latter would require terminal ingredients (a terminal cost that is a control-Lyapunov function plus a terminal set), which this implementation does not have — we note it as future work rather than claim it. (iii) Because the legacy and interface-consistent controllers share the same static-equivalent gains, the criterion certifies *both*, while only the former diverges in practice. That is not a defect of the criterion but a restatement of this paper's thesis: the legacy failure is not a sector-nonlinearity phenomenon that absolute-stability tools can see, but an over-commanding of the *predictive* internal model, for which the causal evidence remains the non-predictive consensus baseline of §6.5.

**Relation to delay-margin theory.** That a networked agreement system's stability margin is governed by the graph Laplacian's spectrum is classical: for consensus dynamics with a single communication delay, the delay margin is set by one "responsible" Laplacian eigenvalue [SipahiQiao11], and recent analyses give it explicitly via the Lambert-$W$ function [Badran25]. Our $\zeta$ is a specialization of this delay-margin-versus-eigenvalue relationship to a *receding-horizon formation-MPC* setting: the "delay" is the identified velocity-loop lag $\tau$ rather than a communication delay, and the coupling enters as an added stiffness $w_f\nu$ in the MPC's own stage cost rather than through a raw agreement protocol. One difference is worth flagging against that lineage: in those results the margin shrinks with the delay, whereas here it does not, because the controller is re-optimized *at* the identified lag (the lag-invariance above). What the lag does erode is the saturation certificate, not the linear margin. Reading the ordering above as an instance of this known structural law — not an isolated observation — both grounds it and delimits it: like those results it is a *linear* margin, necessary but not sufficient for the saturation-driven large-signal instability (§5.4) whose amplitude it does not set. The lineage is theoretical; it does not carry empirical weight here, since the sweep below does not recover the ordering the margin implies.

**A robustness view, and why the divergence is *not* a describing-function limit cycle.** The legacy controller designs its feedback as if velocity were realized instantaneously — i.e. against the nominal velocity-to-position plant $P_0(s)=1/s$ — whereas the true plant is $P(s)=\tfrac{1}{s(\tau s+1)}$. The unmodelled dynamics are the multiplicative model error $\Delta(s)=\tfrac{P(s)}{P_0(s)}-1=\tfrac{1}{\tau s+1}-1=\tfrac{-\tau s}{\tau s+1}$, whose magnitude $|\Delta(j\omega)|=\tfrac{\tau\omega}{\sqrt{1+\tau^2\omega^2}}$ rises monotonically from $0$ at DC to $1$ at high frequency. By the small-gain robust-stability condition, a loop tuned on $P_0$ remains stable on $P$ only while $|\Delta(j\omega)\,T_0(j\omega)|<1$, with $T_0$ the nominal complementary sensitivity; since $|\Delta|\to1$, the surviving margin is set by the closed-loop bandwidth, and the more aggressively the MPC commands — the closer it drives the velocity command toward saturation — the higher that bandwidth and the smaller the margin. This makes precise the qualitative statement of §5.4 that *approaching saturation* is what erodes stability. **A describing-function reading of the same algebra agrees, and is reported only as a consistency check** — harmonic balance is an approximation, so the rigorous statement is Proposition 2 above, not the argument that follows. Closing the loop through the velocity-command saturation and applying harmonic balance, the linear part is $G(j\omega)=\dfrac{K_p+K_v\,j\omega}{j\omega\,(1+j\omega\tau)}$; its imaginary part is $\propto-\omega\,(K_p+K_v\tau\omega^2)<0$ for all $\omega>0$ and its real part is $\propto\big(K_v-K_p\tau\big)=K_2-K_1\tau$ — the same quantity the circle criterion above turns into a certificate — which is positive for every topology considered here under the rigidity spectrum ($0.240$ for an isolated vehicle, $0.099$–$0.121$ for the four formations, at $\tau=0.48$ s), though not for `grid9` under the superseded plain-Laplacian proxy ($-0.022$), so $G(j\omega)$ stays in the open fourth quadrant and *never* meets the negative-real critical locus $-1/N(A)$ of a saturation or relay ($N(A)\in(0,1]$ real). No sustained oscillation is predicted by the static-equivalent controller plus saturation. The observed limit cycle is therefore not accounted for by the static equivalent at all. The causal evidence that it is a *predictive* phenomenon comes from the consensus baseline of §6.5 rather than from this argument; the mechanism it points to is that, because the receding-horizon controller's internal model assumes $\dot v=u$, it persistently over-predicts the velocity it will realize and keeps commanding beyond the achievable value until the command saturates — a windup-type mechanism absent from the equivalent LQR gains, whose amplitude consequently scales with the saturation level $v_{\max}$ (Fig. 4) rather than with any describing-function crossing. This is exactly the mechanism the non-predictive consensus baseline of §6.5 isolates empirically: a memoryless law with the *same* equivalent gains but *no* internal over-prediction does not enter the limit cycle even when driven far deeper into saturation — corroborating, from the stability side, that the pathology lives in the predictive controller's (lag-blind) internal model rather than in the coupling, the plant, or the saturation alone.
